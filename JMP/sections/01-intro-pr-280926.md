# Proof-read: JMP/sections/01-intro.qmd (2026-09-28)

**Target audience:** job-market paper introduction — academic but accessible, written for a first-year econ PhD student or an economist outside public economics/IO. Assessed against that register throughout.

**Overall:** the argument is well built — each paragraph opens with its main idea (descending structure), the four "First/Second/Third/Finally" paragraphs map cleanly onto the topic sentence that introduces them (line 16), and several findings are deliberately echoed almost verbatim across paragraphs (e.g. the productivity/overreporting confound in lines 7 and 20; the lower-elasticities/less-dispersed-productivity finding in lines 13 and 22) — that repetition is appropriate here, not an error, since it reinforces the same claim rather than introducing unwanted variation. The issues below are mostly local: a few overlong sentences, one real terminology slip, and some small consistency/clarity fixes.

## 1. Terminology consistency

- **Line 22 — "tax-motivated misreporting of inputs" should read "overreporting."** Every other mention in the introduction (about 15 instances) uses "overreporting," and line 13 explicitly establishes that only overreporting is possible ("no firm gains by underreporting deductible inputs, so it can only point to overreporting"). "Misreporting" is broader and could be read as including underreporting, which the paper rules out. Fix: *"shows how tax-motivated overreporting of inputs distorts production-function estimates."*

## 2. Sentence length / splitting

Three sentences carry two or three separate claims and would read more easily as two sentences each. Splitting doesn't lose any content — the joining "and" in each case is doing no logical work beyond concatenation.

- **Line 13**, first finding: *"It rejects the absence of overreporting in 9 of the 18 manufacturing industries whose products paid the sales tax, including four of the five largest, which together produce about two fifths of manufacturing output, and it rejects decisively, at the 1 percent level, in seven of them, where unincorporated firms overreport on average 16 percent of their true materials in the median industry."* (61 words)
  → Split after "manufacturing output.": *"It rejects the absence of overreporting in 9 of the 18 manufacturing industries whose products paid the sales tax, including four of the five largest, which together produce about two fifths of manufacturing output. It rejects decisively, at the 1 percent level, in seven of them, where unincorporated firms overreport on average 16 percent of their true materials in the median industry."*

- **Line 13**, second finding: *"Second, ignoring overreporting distorts the production function and, with it, measured productivity: in those seven industries, correcting for overreporting recovers output elasticities of materials consistently lower than uncorrected estimates [@Gandhi2020], and less dispersed and more persistent productivity distributions, with a ratio of the 90th to the 10th percentile about 40 percent lower on average and a half-life of productivity shocks 1.6 to 3.1 times longer."* (67 words, and the parallel list "elasticities … lower … and less dispersed … distributions" is broken up by the intervening citation)
  → *"Second, ignoring overreporting distorts the production function and, with it, measured productivity. In those seven industries, correcting for overreporting recovers output elasticities of materials that are consistently lower than uncorrected estimates [@Gandhi2020], and productivity distributions that are less dispersed and more persistent: the ratio of the 90th to the 10th percentile is about 40 percent lower on average, and the half-life of productivity shocks is 1.6 to 3.1 times longer."*
  (Restating each clause as "X that is/are Y" also fixes the parallelism between the two findings, which the citation currently interrupts.)

- **Line 9**, headline result: *"I find that, after Colombia raised its sales-tax rate in 1983 for most manufacturing industries, while exempt industries remained exempt, overreporting by unincorporated firms in the industries affected by the tax increase rose significantly, by about 9 percentage points of true materials between 1983 and 1987."* — see item 4 below; splitting isn't necessary once the tautology is fixed, but consider a period after "1983 for most manufacturing industries" if it still feels long.

## 3. Awkward or unclear phrasing

- **Line 9 — "while exempt industries remained exempt" is tautological.** It's making a real point (the exempt industries are the untreated group), but as worded it just restates "exempt." Suggested fix: *"after Colombia raised its sales-tax rate in 1983 for most manufacturing industries but left a few industries exempt, overreporting by unincorporated firms in the industries affected by the increase rose significantly…"*

- **Line 13 — "It rejects in neither of the two food industries" is a double negative that takes a re-read.** "Rejects" + "neither" forces the reader to compute "does not reject in either." Suggested fix: *"It fails to reject in either of the two food industries, which were exempt from the sales tax and so gained nothing from the credit on purchases."*

- **Line 20 — "mine does not require the two groups to be alike in size or productivity."** The bare pronoun "mine" (for "my approach"/"my comparison") is a small step removed from its antecedent ("the comparisons for individuals"). Suggested fix: *"Unlike the comparisons for individuals, my approach does not require the two groups to be alike in size or productivity…"*

- **Line 18 — "firms that sell to other firms have little room on sales and more on costs" leaves the parallelism to be inferred.** Suggested fix: *"firms that sell to other firms have little room to misreport on the sales side and more on the cost side."*

- **Line 16 — "a response the literature … has mostly seen through individuals' reported income."** "Seen through" is a slightly loose metaphor for "studied by way of." Suggested fix: *"…a response that the literature on taxes and revenue has mostly studied through individuals' reported income."*

- **Line 16 — "has combined all different responses to the rate…into a single statistic."** "all different" reads as a minor grammatical stumble. Suggested fix: *"has combined all the different responses to the rate, evasion included, into a single statistic."*

## 4. Passive voice (optional — flagging per the skill's active-voice check, not all need changing)

- **Line 11 — "Corporations are closely monitored by the government and face the market's reaction to tax aggressiveness."** A clean active-voice candidate: *"The government closely monitors corporations, and they face the market's reaction to tax aggressiveness, which keeps them from overreporting."*
- **Line 20 — "The same comparison has also been used to measure how evasion responds to a tax reform."** Agentless passive with a recoverable actor (researchers). Optional: *"Researchers have also used the same comparison to measure how evasion responds to a tax reform."* Lower priority than the line-11 case, since the passive here isn't creating ambiguity.
- **Line 20 — "employees, whose income is reported by their employers."** Recommend leaving as is: the passive keeps "employees" as the topic across the clause, which an active rewrite ("whose employers report their income") would disrupt.

## 5. Numeral and punctuation style consistency

- **Serial (Oxford) comma used inconsistently.** Line 5: *"Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, Poland and Slovakia"* (no comma before "and"). Line 18: *"confidential, available for few countries, and accessible only to selected researchers"* (comma before "and"). Pick one convention; since line 18 (and standard academic style) uses the serial comma, the simplest fix is adding it at line 5: *"Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, Poland, and Slovakia."*

- **Industry counts mix numerals and spelled-out numbers within the same sentence.** Line 13: *"9 of the 18 manufacturing industries…including four of the five largest…in seven of them"*; later, *"neither of the two food industries."* These are all the same kind of quantity (counts of industries, parallel to the numeral-heavy statistical style used everywhere else in the intro — "9 percentage points," "5 percent," "1 percent level"). Recommend converting all to numerals for consistency: *"9 of the 18…4 of the 5 largest…7 of them…neither of the 2 food industries."* (The structural counts "three findings," "three strands," used to organize the intro's own argument rather than to report a sample statistic, are a different category and are fine spelled out.)

## 6. Acronym/jargon at first use

- **Line 5 — "VAT" is used ("VAT compliance gap") without ever spelling out the acronym**, even though "value-added taxes" appears earlier in the same paragraph. Suggested fix: *"Sales and value-added taxes (VAT) that credit purchases reward firms…"* so the later "VAT compliance gap" is unambiguous to a reader skimming past the first sentence.
- **Line 11 — "Deconvolution" is introduced with no gloss**, and per the target-audience convention (build intuition before formalism) a first-year PhD reader outside this subfield may not know the term. Optional one-clause fix: *"Deconvolution — a technique that recovers a distribution net of measurement noise — then separates the distribution of overreporting from that of the measurement error…"*
