# Proof-read — JMP introduction, paragraph 2 (P)

File: `JMP/sections/01-intro.qmd`, line 98 (paragraph starting "How tax revenue responds to tax rates"). Date: 24 September 2026.

## Current text

> How tax revenue responds to tax rates has been measured for individuals' reported income, whose elasticity bundles real responses with evasion and avoidance [@Saez2012; @Chetty2009], and theory implies that rates should be lower where evasion responds more to them [@Cremer1993]. For firms, the evidence comes mainly from bunching at kinks and notches in the tax schedule [@Best2015] and from misreported imports [@FismanWei2004]. How firms' overreporting of inputs, the fraud that the credit on purchases in sales and value-added taxes invites, responds to the tax rate has not been estimated. The difficulty is that overreported inputs cannot be seen in the data: a firm reporting high input use for its output may be overreporting, or it may simply be unproductive.

## Findings

### 1. The main idea is buried (structure)

The paragraph's point is the contrast "a lot is known about how reported income responds to tax rates; little about how firms' input overreporting does". That contrast only emerges in sentence 3. A reader skimming the first sentence gets the literature, not the gap.

**Suggestion:** open with the contrast, then give the evidence for each half, then the reason the gap exists.

### 2. Sentence 1 mixes empirics and theory and runs too long (sentence structure)

Sentence 1 joins three ideas: what has been measured (the revenue response for individuals), a qualification of it ("whose elasticity bundles…"), and a theoretical result (Cremer and Gahvari), linked by "and". The theory clause has a different subject and a different kind of claim.

**Suggestion:** split into one sentence on the evidence for individuals and a separate sentence on theory, placed after the evidence on firms.

### 3. Tone toward the elasticity-of-taxable-income literature (word choice)

"Whose elasticity bundles real responses with evasion and avoidance" reads as a criticism of Saez, Slemrod and Giertz, and of Chetty. It is in fact a point these authors make themselves: Chetty (2009) shows that evasion and avoidance change how the elasticity maps into welfare. Framing it as their finding is both more accurate and more generous.

**Suggestion:** "…and shows that this response includes evasion and avoidance as well as changes in real activity [@Saez2012; @Chetty2009]."

### 4. "Has not been estimated" is stronger than what can be verified (claim)

An unqualified "has not been estimated" invites a referee to find the exception. The honest claim is about what the author knows.

**Suggestion:** "to my knowledge, no study has estimated…".

### 5. The apposition in sentence 3 overloads the subject (sentence structure)

"How firms' overreporting of inputs, the fraud that the credit on purchases in sales and value-added taxes invites, responds to the tax rate" puts a 16-word apposition between the subject and its verb.

**Suggestion:** move the subject up front: "no study has estimated how firms' overreporting of inputs, a form of fraud that the credit on purchases makes attractive, responds to the tax rate." The apposition is shorter, and it now sits in the object rather than between subject and verb.

### 6. "The evidence" in sentence 2 is ambiguous (word accuracy)

"For firms, the evidence comes mainly from…": evidence of what? After a sentence about individuals and theory, the referent is unclear.

**Suggestion:** "For firms, evidence on responses to tax rates comes mainly from…".

### 7. "The difficulty is" and "cannot be seen" (verbs and precision)

"The difficulty is that…" uses "to be" where a more direct claim is available, and presents the missing observability as the only reason. "Cannot be seen in the data" is informal next to the rest of the paragraph.

**Suggestion:** "One reason is that overreported inputs are not observed in the data: …". "One reason" is also more honest, since other obstacles exist (for example, few statutory rate changes).

## Suggested revision

> Economists know a good deal about how reported income responds to tax rates, but much less about how firms' overreporting of inputs does. For individuals, a large literature estimates how reported income responds to the tax rate and shows that this response includes evasion and avoidance as well as changes in real activity [@Saez2012; @Chetty2009]. For firms, evidence on responses to tax rates comes mainly from bunching at kinks and notches in the tax schedule [@Best2015] and from misreported imports [@FismanWei2004]. Theory adds a reason to care: optimal commodity-tax rates are lower where evasion responds more to the rate [@Cremer1993]. One reason is that overreported inputs are not observed in the data: a firm reporting high input use for its output may be overreporting, or it may simply be unproductive.

# Abstract (RAP 2) — 25 September 2026

## Superseded abstract (testing-first), kept for reference

> I propose a novel strategy to estimate corporate cost-overreporting tax evasion. Employing a structural production function and a subset of truth-reporting firms, I first investigate potential tax evasion by testing the presence of cost overreporting, where the statistically significant differences on the output elasticities of the overreported input in the correct direction are interpreted as evidence of tax evasion. Using a well-known dataset of Colombian manufacturing firms, I detect overreporting in 9 of the 20 manufacturing industries tested, including four of the five largest, which together produce about half of manufacturing output; with 20 tests at the 10 percent level, chance alone would produce about two rejections. Where the test cannot reject the absence of overreporting, the result is inconclusive. Second, I recover the distribution of the overreporting share of true materials. In the five industries with the strongest evidence, firms overreport between 11 and 25 percent of their true materials on average. The density estimates sugget that most firms evade modestly with few aggresive evaders. Third, leveraging a 1983 fiscal reform that raised the sales tax rate for a subset of industries while cutting income-tax rates, I find that overreporting rose after the reform where the net incentive rose most: in industries whose sales tax increased, and among limited liability companies, whose smaller income-tax cut did little to offset it. > Sales and value-added taxes that credit purchases reward firms for overreporting their inputs: every fictitious dollar of deductible inputs lowers their tax liability, and thus the higher the rate, the larger the reward.
