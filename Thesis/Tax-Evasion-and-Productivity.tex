\chapter*{Abstract}\label{abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

\markboth{Abstract}{Abstract}

I propose a novel strategy to estimate corporate cost-overreporting tax
evasion. Employing a structural production function and a subset of
truth-reporting firms, I first investigate potential tax evasion by
testing the presence of cost overreporting, where the statistically
significant differences on the output elasticities of the overreported
input in the correct direction are interpreted as evidence of tax
evasion. Using a well-known dataset, I find evidence suggesting that
cost overreporting in most manufacturing industries. Second, I recover
the distribution of the overreporting share of true materials. Cost
overreporting ranges in average from to percent. The density estimates
sugget that most firms evade modestly with few aggresive evaders. Third,
leveraging a fiscal policy change where the sales tax rate increased for
a subset of industries while the tax rate remained the same for others.
I find that, in industries with tax increases, firm raised their
overreported share significantly. Fourth, I show how to recover the
production function paramaters and productivity in the presence of tax
evasion. My results indicate that ignoring cost overreporting leads to
consistently larger output elasticities of ovrrerported inputs. In
addition, I find non-trivial differences in the productivity
distributions with respect the naive estimation. Finally, using a simple
model, I estimate how changes in tax rate affect government revenue
around the current equilibrium. I find that a tax increase of 0.5\% on
all purchases will significantly reduce tax revenues while a cut of 8\%
would be needed to increase revenue significantly from observed state.

\chapter{Introduction}\label{sec-intro}

Cost overreporting arises when firms acquire false invoices to claim
additional tax deductions on value-added (VAT) and corporate income
taxes (CIT). According to the OECD's document OECD (2017), cost
overreporting --- also known as ``fake invoicing'', ``ghost firms'',
``invoice mills'', or ``missing traders''--- permeates internationally.

\chapter{A Model of Tax Evasion through Input
Overreporting}\label{sec-model}

\section{Setup}\label{setup}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Firm chooses \(K_{it},L_{it},M_{it}\) (true materials) and
  \(e_{it}\ge0\) (overreported materials, \(M^*_{it}=M_{it}+e_{it}\) is
  what's reported to the tax authority) to maximize expected profit.
\item
  Detection: à la Allingham and Sandmo (1972) -- tax authority audits
  with probability \(q(e_{it})\), always catches evasion if audited (no
  partial detection). \(q(e_{it})\in[0,1]\), increasing, \(q(0)=0\).
\item
  If caught, the firm repays the excess credit claimed on \(e_{it}\),
  plus a penalty multiplier \((1+\phi)\) (usually \(\phi=0\)).
\item
  Cost of evasion \(\kappa_{it}(e_{it},\omega_{it})\): resources
  (e.g.~manager's time) diverted from production to concealment.
  Increasing in \(e_{it}\); assumed independent of \(M_{it}\) (choosing
  how much to overreport doesn't enter the materials choice or vice
  versa -- \(M\perp e\mid\omega\)).
\item
  Production:
  \(Y_{it}=K_{it}^{\alpha_K}L_{it}^{\alpha_L}M_{it}^{\beta}\exp(\omega_{it}+\varepsilon_{it})\),
  \(\omega\) = productivity (state variable, AR(1) later),
  \(\varepsilon\) = ex-post shock, \(E[\exp\varepsilon]=1\).
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-profit}{
\begin{aligned}
  \max_{K,L,M,e} \; \mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}] = &(1-\tau)\big(P_t\mathbb{E}[Y_{it}]-\rho_tM_{it}\big)-r_tK_{it}-w_tL_{it}\\
  &+\big[1-q(e_{it})\big]\rho_t\tau e_{it}-q(e_{it})\phi\rho_t\tau e_{it}-\kappa_{it}(e_{it},\omega_{it})
\end{aligned}
}\end{equation}

subject to the production function above. (\(\rho_t\) = materials price,
\(\tau\) = tax rate -- single rate in this chapter, see SS2.6.)

\section{First-order conditions}\label{first-order-conditions}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Materials FOC} (standard, static, doesn't involve \(e\)):
  \begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-foc-m}{
  \beta K_{it}^{\alpha_K}L_{it}^{\alpha_L}M_{it}^{-(1-\beta)}\exp(\omega_{it})=\rho_t/P_t
  }\end{equation}
\item
  \textbf{Evasion FOC} (interior solution):
  \begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-foc-e}{
  \tau\rho_t\big(1-(1+\phi)(q(e_{it})+q'(e_{it})e_{it})\big)=\partial\kappa_{it}(e_{it},\omega_{it})/\partial e_{it}
  }\end{equation}
\item
  \textbf{Corner solution:} some firms optimally choose \(e_{it}=0\) --
  marginal cost exceeds marginal benefit at every \(e>0\). For these
  firms Equation~\ref{eq-foc-e} becomes an inequality,
  \(\tau\rho_t\le\partial\kappa_{it}(0,\omega_{it})/\partial e_{it}\).

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \textbf{Corporations are constrained non-evaders}: audited
    statements / third-party reporting make \(e=0\) a hard institutional
    constraint for them, not a corner outcome of Equation~\ref{eq-foc-e}
    -- used only in the first stage (ch.~4-5), outside the evasion FOC
    entirely. No exogenous cost shifter \(\delta_C\) for corporations
    (considered, rejected: can't classify corner firms exogenously; the
    U-shaped \(\kappa\) below handles both tails through the interior
    channel alone).
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section{Second-order condition}\label{second-order-condition}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Profit's Hessian is block-diagonal (\(e\) separate from \(K,L,M\)
  since \(M\perp e\)) and the production function is strictly concave,
  so the SOC reduces to one condition:
  \(\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]/\partial e_{it}^2<0\).
\item
  Simplifies (with \(\phi=0\)) to a restriction on the curvature of
  \(q\) alone: \begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-soc}{
  -\frac{q''(e_{it})}{q'(e_{it})}e_{it}<2
  }\end{equation}
\item
  \textbf{Linear \(q\) always satisfies this} (\(q''=0\)). Convex always
  satisfies it too. Concave needs ``not too concave.''
\end{itemize}

\section{Detection function: three candidates, one
headline}\label{detection-function-three-candidates-one-headline}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Define the \textbf{log marginal benefit}
  \(h(e)\equiv\ln(\tau\rho_t)+\ln(1-q(e)-q'(e)e)\) (with \(\phi=0\));
  the FOC is \(h(e)=\ln(\partial\kappa/\partial e)\). Keep the symbol
  \(h\) generic here -- it's reused with each functional form
  substituted in.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Linear \(q=\min\{\lambda e,1\}\) is the headline
  specification.} Audit-threshold interpretation: tax authority draws
  \(T\sim U[0,1/\lambda]\), audits if \(e\ge T\); \(1/\lambda\) =
  largest overreporting any audit tolerates, mean threshold
  \(1/2\lambda\). At the FOC ceiling the most aggressive evader is
  caught with probability exactly 50\% -- a coin flip.
\item
  Concave (exponential) was tested as a robustness check for a specific
  estimation problem (extreme-evader leverage in \(\hat\lambda\),
  ch.~6/8) and rejected: only delays the ceiling by a fixed
  \textasciitilde2x factor, not enough to matter. Convex was not
  estimated (would only tighten the ceiling further, the wrong direction
  for that problem).
\item
  \textbf{Report detection probability in relative terms, not absolute.}
  Without independent audit-rate data, \(q\)'s absolute level isn't
  calibratable -- a skeptic can dispute \(\hat\lambda\). But under
  linear \(q\), \(q(e_A)/q(e_B)=e_A/e_B\) is exactly
  \(\hat\lambda\)-invariant, so relative detection risk against a
  benchmark (e.g.~the median evader) is a robust, assumption-light
  readout of the evasion distribution alone. (Table with real numbers:
  ch.~8.)
\end{itemize}

\section{Cost of evasion}\label{cost-of-evasion}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(\kappa_{it}(e_{it},\omega_{it})=e_{it}\exp\{\delta_0-\delta_1\omega_{it}+\delta_2\omega_{it}^2+\psi_{it}\}\)
  -- linear in \(e\) (tractable: gives \(e^*\) in closed form under
  linear \(q\)), \textbf{convex in \(\omega\)} (minimum at
  \(\omega^*=\delta_1/2\delta_2\)).
\item
  Economic rationale for the convexity: more productive firms have more
  resources to hide evasion in, but the most productive firms also have
  the best access to \emph{legal} avoidance, so illegal evasion costs
  them more at the margin -- both tails of the productivity distribution
  evade less than the middle.
\item
  \(\psi_{it}\): idiosyncratic cost shock, the source of evasion
  heterogeneity beyond what \(\omega\) explains.
\item
  Substituting linear \(q\) and this \(\kappa\) into
  Equation~\ref{eq-foc-e} and taking logs:
  \begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-foc-psi}{
  \psi_{it}=h(e_{it})-\delta_0+\delta_1\omega_{it}-\delta_2\omega_{it}^2,\qquad h(e_{it})=\ln(\tau\rho_t)+\ln(1-2\lambda e_{it})
  }\end{equation} This is the equation every stage-2 estimator (ch.~6,
  8) is built from.
\item
  \(q\) and \(\kappa\) are not separately identified without independent
  audit data; functional-form assumptions here are what let estimation
  reproduce the observed evasion-by-productivity pattern (ch.~7's
  motivating fact, Carrillo et al. (2022)).
\end{itemize}

\section{Closing note: the two-tax-rate
generalization}\label{closing-note-the-two-tax-rate-generalization}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Everything above assumes a single tax rate \(\tau\). The data
  separately report a sales-side rate \(\tau_S\) and a purchases-side
  rate \(\tau_P\); the model generalizes to \(\tau_S\ne\tau_P\) with the
  firm claiming a purchases-tax credit on \(M^*=M+e\) while owing sales
  tax on revenue at \(\tau_S\).
\item
  \textbf{Only \(\tau_P\) ever enters the evasion FOC} -- re-deriving
  Equation~\ref{eq-foc-e} under two rates, the \(\tau_S\)-revenue term
  doesn't involve \(e\) and drops out of \(\partial\pi/\partial e\)
  entirely. The single-\(\tau\) model used above is exactly the special
  case \(\tau_S=\tau_P\), not a different or wrong model.
\item
  This generalization is used only in ch.~8, where \(\tau_P\) is the
  rate actually shocked in the counterfactual. It is \emph{not} applied
  to the first stage in ch.~4-6: the evasion-FOC-side residual used
  there never needed tax-splitting to begin with, and the one channel
  that is exposed (materials-FOC/production identity, feeding
  \(\hat\beta\)) is measured to move \(\hat\beta\) by at most 0.012
  across the headline industries (Table~\ref{tbl-beta-net-tax}) --
  small, though not yet re-confirmed downstream in the ELVIS estimates
  themselves (stated explicitly as a limitation in ch.~8).
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Five types in the data; four kept in the analysis (Corporation,
  Limited Liability Company, Partnership, Proprietorship). Others
  (cooperatives, community/state enterprises) excluded --- not
  profit-driven, no evasion incentive of the kind modelled.
\item
  Definitions (safe to copy from \texttt{80-colombia.qmd}, institutional
  facts, not model output):

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \textbf{Corporation} (\emph{sociedad anónima}): capital association,
    \(N\ge5\) shareholders, tradable shares, liability limited to
    capital, supervised by the Superintendent of Corporations with a
    mandatory auditor.
  \item
    \textbf{Limited liability company} (\emph{sociedad de
    responsabilidad limitada}): \(2\le N\le20\) (25 in the 1992 EAM
    documentation), non-tradable shares, liability limited to capital.
  \item
    \textbf{Partnership} (\emph{sociedad colectiva} and variants):
    \(N\ge2\), full/joint liability, not a capital association.
  \item
    \textbf{Proprietorship}: single individual, full liability.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Corporate Income Tax (CIT) by type: Corporations 40\% on distributed
  dividends; Partnerships and LLCs 20\% on profits (whether distributed
  or not); Proprietorships on the graduated individual schedule (56
  brackets, 0.5\%-51\%).
\item
  Since 1974, a minimum presumptive income (8\% of net wealth) applied

\section{Sales tax}\label{sec-setting-st}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Targeted manufacturing (finished goods, imports). Since 1974, credit
  for tax paid on purchases (except capital goods) --- a VAT in
  substance.
\item
  Basic rate 15\%; preferential 6\% for clothing, footwear,
  popular-housing inputs; 35\% on luxury goods. Exports, foodstuffs,
  drugs, textbooks excluded from the start, along with
  capital/agricultural inputs and transport/agricultural equipment.
\item
  This is the rate the 1983 reform changed (Chapter~\ref{sec-fiscal}):
  most ST-liable industries moved from 6\% to 10\%; ST-exempt industries
  stayed exempt. State this here as background; the reform and its
  effect on evasion belong to Chapter~\ref{sec-fiscal}, not here.
\item
  \textbf{The 1983 reform: debate, enactment, and effective date.}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \emph{Debate (1981--1983).} The Bird--Wiesner Mission (1978--1981),
    a technical mission on Colombia's intergovernmental finances led by
    Richard Bird and Eduardo Wiesner, reported in 1981 (Junguito and
    Rincón 2004). Among its recommendations: better tax administration
    to reduce evasion, stronger collection tools, wider withholding, and
    more auditing; the 1983 reforms followed (Bolaños Bolaños 2019).
  \item
    \emph{Enactment (end of 1983).} Using the extraordinary powers of
    art. 53 of Ley 9 de 1983, the government issued Decreto 3541 on 29
    December 1983 (Diario Oficial No.~36.452, 18 January 1984). It set
    the general sales-tax rate at 10\% (art. 6) and extended the tax to
    the retail level as a value-added tax (Presidencia de la República
    de Colombia 1983; Perry and Triana 1990).
  \item
    \emph{Effective date (1 April 1984).} The pre-reform sales tax
    applied until 31 March 1984; the value-added tax applied from 1
    April 1984 (art. 92 of Decreto 3541) (Corte Constitucional de
    Colombia 2016). Perry and Triana (1990) likewise date the new
    structure to ``the reforms of April 1984''. So \textbf{1983 is the
    last full pre-reform year and 1984 is a partial year}.
  \item
    \emph{1990 reform.} Ley 49 de 1990 (28 December 1990) raised the
    general rate to 12\% from 1 January 1991 (art. 26) (Congreso de la
    República de Colombia 1990).
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{The 1983 reform also cut income-tax rates} (Perry and Cárdenas
  1986, vol. 1, p.~36; Ocampo and Perry 1983). Ley 9 de 1983 lowered the
  individual schedule, and thus the rates faced by Proprietorships, to
  offset the rise in effective rates caused by inflation since 1974

  to 49\%. LLCs' rate fell from 20\% to 18\%, to compensate them for
  being brought under the presumptive income regime by the same law.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Fiscal Policy Evaluation: The 1983 Reform}\label{sec-fiscal}

Incentives to evade taxes may change over time due to changes in fiscal
policy. In Colombia, the reform of 1983 increased, from 6\% to 10\%, the
Sales Tax (ST) rate of most manufacturing industries. In this section, I
explore whether firms reacted to the increase in the tax rate by
increasing evasion.

\section{The reform and the incentives}\label{sec-reform}

In 1983, the government tried to homogenize the sales tax rates across
industries, which led to some industries facing higher sales tax rates
while others faced lower rates or were exempted. The 1983 reform
relatively unified the sales tax rates by combining previously taxed
goods at 6 and 15\% into 10\%. The number of the products and services
that were levied expanded. The sales tax was extended to the retail
level, with a \emph{simplified system} being made available to small
retailers to ease compliance costs and the administrative burden.

In the model, the sales tax rate shifts the marginal benefit of
overreporting materials: a higher \(\tau\) raises the credit claimed on
each peso of fictitious inputs (Chapter~\ref{sec-model}). The model has
no income tax, but the same logic extends to it: overreported inputs
also lower reported profits, and with them the income-tax base. An
increase in either rate therefore raises the incentive to overreport
inputs, and a decrease lowers it. Since the 1983 reform raised the sales
tax for most industries while cutting income-tax rates, its net effect
on evasion is ambiguous a priori.

The 1983 fiscal policy changed incentives \emph{across} industries and
\emph{within} industries. Across industries, the sales tax rate
increased for most ST-liable industries, while ST-exempt industries
remained exempt. Within industries, the income tax changed by juridical
organization (JO), and in the opposite direction. \emph{Ley 9 de 1983}
cut individual income-tax rates by 4.6 percentage points on average
across income brackets relative to the 1982 schedule, and thus
proprietorships faced lower rates (Perry and Cárdenas 1986). LLCs
received a smaller cut, from 20\% to 18\% (Perry and Cárdenas 1986). In
ST-liable industries, the net incentive is therefore ambiguous for both:
proprietorships were compensated for much of the sales-tax increase
through the income tax, while LLCs were insufficiently compensated. In
ST-exempt industries, the income-tax cut alone lowered the incentive for
proprietorships and changed it little for LLCs, whose cut was small.

\section{Empirical approach}\label{sec-fiscal-approach}

From the materials FOC, for corporations, the truth-reporters, \(i\) in
industry \(j\) at year \(t\), the log share of materials' cost over
sales is

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-fiscal-corp}{
s_{ijt}^{C} = \ln\beta_j-\varepsilon_{ijt}
}\end{equation}

where \(s_{ijt}\equiv\ln(\rho_tM^*_{ijt}/P_tY_{ijt})\) is the log share
of materials' cost over sales, \(\beta_j\) is the output elasticity of
materials of industry \(j\), and \(\varepsilon_{ijt}\) is the error
term. For unincorporated firms, the evaders,

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-fiscal-unincorp}{
s_{ijt}^{N} = \ln\beta_j + u_{ijt} - \varepsilon_{ijt}
}\end{equation}

where \(u_{ijt}=\ln(1+e_{ijt}/M_{ijt})\) is the overreporting of
materials' cost due to tax evasion.

Let \(D_{ijt}^N\) be a dummy indicating whether the firm is an
unincorporated firm. Then,

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-fiscal-stack}{
\begin{aligned}
  (1-D_{ijt}^N)s_{ijt} + D_{ijt}^N s_{ijt} =& (1-D_{ijt}^N)(\ln\beta_j-\varepsilon_{ijt}) + D_{ijt}^N \big(\ln\beta_j+u_{ijt}  - \varepsilon_{ijt}\big) \\
    s_{ijt} =& \ln\beta_j + D_{ijt}^N  u_{ijt} - \varepsilon_{ijt}
\end{aligned}
}\end{equation}

Taking expectations,

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-fiscal-mean}{
E[s_{ijt}|D^{N}=0,j]=\ln\beta_j, \qquad E[s_{ijt}|D^{N}=1,j]-E[s_{ijt}|D^{N}=0,j]= \mu
}\end{equation}

identifies the average overreporting level,
\(\mu\equiv E[u_{ijt}\mid D^N_{ijt}=1]\), a population expectation; its
estimate is \(\hat\mu\).

In the case of the fiscal policy changes, I am interested in the changes
in average tax evasion after the 1983 fiscal reform. Taking the
difference of the expectations between year \(t'\) and \(t=1983\),

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-fiscal-diff}{
\begin{aligned}
    s_{ijt'} - s_{ij,1983}
     &= D_{ijt}^N  (u_{ijt'} - u_{ij,1983}) - (\varepsilon_{ijt'} - \varepsilon_{ij,1983}) \\
    E[s_{ijt'}-s_{ij,1983}|D^{N}=1] &= \mu_{t'} - \mu_{1983} \equiv \Delta\mu_{t'}
\end{aligned}
}\end{equation}

where \(\mu_t\) is the annual average tax evasion of year \(t\), and
\(\Delta\mu_{t}\) is the change in average tax evasion between year
\(t\) and 1983, the last full year before the reform took effect
(Section~\ref{sec-setting-st}).

The identifying assumptions are explicit in
Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-stack} and Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-diff}: (i)
corporations do not overreport (Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-corp}); (ii)
firms within an industry share a common Cobb-Douglas technology, with a
constant output elasticity of materials \(\beta_j\)
(Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-corp} and Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-unincorp});
and (iii) the ex-post shock \(\varepsilon_{ijt}\) is independent of
evasion, true materials and juridical organization, with
\(E[\varepsilon_{ijt}\mid D^N_{ijt},t]=0\).

Even though Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-diff} resembles a traditional
difference-in-differences design, the result does not require parallel
trends for identification. Under (i)-(iii),
Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-stack} and Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-diff} show
that the difference between unincorporated firms and corporations in
year \(t\) is the average overreporting itself, \(\mu_t\), and changes
in \(\mu_t\) over time, including before 1983, are changes in evasion.

To estimate Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-stack}, I use the following
regression specification,

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-fiscal-lvl-reg}{
    s_{ijt} = \ln\beta_j + \sum_{t=1981}^{1991} \mu_t\, D_{ijt}^N\,\mathbb 1[\text{year}=t] + \zeta_{ijt}
}\end{equation}

where \(\mu_{t}\) is the average tax evasion of unincorporated firms in
year \(t\); \(\ln\beta_j\) enter as industry fixed effects, estimated
jointly with the \(\mu_t\), so no first-stage estimate enters the
regression and its standard errors need no bootstrap or
generated-regressor correction; and
\(\zeta_{ijt}=\nu_{ijt}-\varepsilon_{ijt}\), where
\(u_{ijt} = \mu_t + \nu_{ijt}\) and \(\nu_{ijt}\) is the idiosyncratic
component of tax evasion that is not captured by the year dummies.
Likewise, I estimate Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-diff} with the following
regression,

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-fiscal-diff-reg}{
    s_{ijt} = \ln\beta_j + \mu\, D_{ijt}^N+\sum_{t=1981}^{1991} \mathbb{1}[t\neq1983]\,\Delta\mu_t\, D_{ijt}^N\,\mathbb 1[\text{year}=t] + \zeta_{ijt}
}\end{equation}

where \(\Delta\mu_{t}\) is the difference in tax evasion of
unincorporated firms between year \(t\) and 1983, the last full year
before the reform took effect, and \(\mu\) is the average tax evasion of
unincorporated firms in 1983.

The 1983 fiscal reform changed incentives to evade taxes over time
\emph{differently} for firms in ST-liable and ST-exempt industries. Most
ST-liable industries faced an ST rate \emph{increase} from 6\% to 10\%,
while ST-exempt industries \emph{remained exempt} before and after the
reform. Note that firms in \emph{all} industries are subject to the CIT,
which also generates incentives to evade taxes by overreporting input
costs: lower reported profits due to higher input costs reduce the tax
base, and thus the tax liability. Therefore, CIT and ST generate tax
evasion incentives for firms in ST-liable industries, while only CIT
does for firms in ST-exempt industries. For unincorporated firms,

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-fiscal-noncorp}{
\begin{aligned}
s_{ijt}^{N} &= \ln\beta_j + u^{ST}_{ijt} + u^{CIT}_{ijt}-\varepsilon_{ijt} & j &\in \text{ST-Liable} \\
s_{ijt}^{N} &= \ln\beta_j + u^{CIT}_{ijt}-\varepsilon_{ijt} & j &\in \text{ST-Exempt}
\end{aligned}
}\end{equation}

where \(u^{ST}_{ijt}\) and \(u^{CIT}_{ijt}\) stand for the overreporting
due to ST and CIT, respectively. Let \(D^E_{ijt}\) be a dummy indicating
whether the industry is ST-exempt and \(D^{L}_{ijt}\) a dummy indicating
whether the industry is ST-liable, such that
\(D^E_{ijt} + D^{L}_{ijt} = 1\). Combining Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-corp}
with Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-noncorp},

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-fiscal-lvl-split}{
    s_{ijt} = \ln\beta_j + D_{ijt}^N D^E_{ijt}\, u^{CIT}_{ijt} + D_{ijt}^N D^{L}_{ijt} (u^{ST}_{ijt} + u^{CIT}_{ijt}) - \varepsilon_{ijt}
}\end{equation}

Taking expectations,

\[
\begin{aligned}
E[s_{ijt}|D^{N}=1,D^E=1,j]-\ln\beta_j &= \mu^{CIT}_E \\
E[s_{ijt}|D^{N}=1,D^{L}=1,j]-\ln\beta_j &= \mu^{ST}_{L}+\mu^{CIT}_{L}\equiv\mu^{ST+CIT}_{L}
\end{aligned}
\]

identifies the average overreporting by firms in ST-exempt industries,
\(\mu^{CIT}_E\), and in ST-liable industries, \(\mu^{ST+CIT}_{L}\). If I
assume \(\mu^{CIT}_E = \mu^{CIT}_{L}\), I can also identify the
overreporting due to ST, \(\mu^{ST}_{L}\). Since CIT rates depend on the
juridical organization and not on the industry, I assume that CIT
generates the same incentives to evade taxes across industries, whether
they are exempt of ST or not. Because I aggregate different juridical
organizations in exempt and liable industries into unincorporated firms,
this assumption might break if the composition of juridical
organizations is non-trivially different between exempt and liable
industries. The regression versions of Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-lvl-reg}
and Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-diff-reg} interact the year dummies with
\(D^N_{ijt}D^E_{ijt}\) and \(D^N_{ijt}D^L_{ijt}\), giving one path per
industry type, \(\mu_{E,t}\) and \(\mu_{L,t}\) (and \(\Delta\mu_{E,t}\),
\(\Delta\mu_{L,t}\) relative to 1983). Splitting unincorporated firms
further by JO, into LLCs and proprietorships, gives four such paths.

\subsection{Changes in true materials cannot explain the
pattern}\label{sec-fiscal-m}

Since \(u_{ijt}=\ln(1+e_{ijt}/M_{ijt})\), the empirical exercise remains
valid as long as changes in \(s\) are not explained by changes in \(M\)
(or \(\varepsilon\)), but only by \(e\) or \(e/M\): changes in the proxy
should be explained by changes to the incentives to adjust
overreporting, not by firms adjusting their optimal choice of true
inputs.

The alternative argument is that changes in true inputs explain the
increase in the overreporting share. Suppose \(e\) is unchanged. Then,
after the reform, unincorporated firms would have to lower their true
inputs, by either an increase in relative prices or productivity shocks.
Productivity shocks are unrelated to tax rates, so this is unlikely.
According to the FOC, taxes do not show in the optimal choice of firms.
Besides, the increase after the reform is gradual, while the reform made
only a one-time change. So this alternative argument does not hold. The
evasion channel is more direct: an increase in the sales tax rate leads
to an increase in the overreporting share because a higher tax rate
increases the marginal benefit of overreporting.

After the fiscal reform of 1983 increased the sales tax rate, tax
evasion through input overreporting increased gradually
(Figure~\ref{fig-fiscal-all}, Table~\ref{tbl-fiscal-all}). Input
overreporting reached an average of 8\% in 1987, where it stabilized

The standard errors of the levels and of the differences over time look
very different. In levels, the large standard errors suggest that tax
evasion is widely heterogeneous across firms. This heterogeneity may
come from differences in the tax rate due to firm characteristics
(industry, JO, and exports), in the (perceived) probability of detection
(the model assumes risk neutrality, but firms might have different
perceived probabilities), and in the cost of evasion across firms. The
differences over time, however, are tight for a mechanical reason: they
are differences, not levels. Every year's level \(\hat\mu_t\) is
estimated against the same baseline (corporations pooled over all years,
and the same industry fixed effects), so estimation error in that
baseline enters every year with the same sign, and the level estimates
are strongly positively correlated. Since
\(\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu_t-\hat\mu_{1983})=\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu_t)+\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu_{1983})-2\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\mu_t,\hat\mu_{1983})\),
this shared error cancels in the differences. The correlation affects
inference only, not the consistency of the estimates: the regressors are
group-by-year dummies, and
\(E[\zeta_{ijt}\mid D^N_{ijt},\text{year}]=0\) because \(\varepsilon\)
is independent of \((e,M)\) and \(\nu_{ijt}\) is, by construction, the
deviation from the group-year mean. Standard errors are two-way
clustered by plant and year.

\subsection{Overreporting before the reform}\label{sec-fiscal-pre}

Corporations are a clean comparison group that will not react to the
policy changes, because the assumption is that these firms do not evade
taxes by overreporting. Thus, I can interpret the estimates as the
change in overreporting relative to 1983, the last full year before the
reform took effect.

The policy changes of the time were the result of a public debate
following the 1981 report of the Bird--Wiesner Mission, a technical
mission on Colombia's intergovernmental finances led by Richard Bird
(international tax expert) and Eduardo Wiesner (Colombian economist)
that recommended better tax administration to reduce evasion (Junguito
and Rincón 2004; Bolaños Bolaños 2019). The reform was enacted at the
end of December 1983 and took effect on 1 April 1984
(Section~\ref{sec-setting-st}). These changes aimed to reduce the fiscal
deficit without resorting to money financing and to reactivate
investment through tax incentives, while broadening the tax base and
simplifying collection (Sánchez Torres and Gutiérrez Sourdis 1994).

A plausible scenario is that firms could have reduced their
overreporting during the public debate as a way to signal compliance and
avoid further tax increases, and then reverted their behaviour after the
reform was enacted. If this is the case, then part of the increase in
overreporting after 1983 is a return to previous levels of tax evasion.
More data points before 1981 would help to clarify this issue.

would lead to lower incentives to evade taxes. However, since the
reduction in CIT is small, it is not unreasonable to find no evidence of
significant changes in tax evasion for these industries. In ST-liable
industries, LLCs faced two opposing effects: the increase in sales tax
rates, which would increase incentives to evade taxes, and the decrease
in CIT, which would decrease incentives to evade taxes. The evidence
suggests that the increase in sales tax rates dominates the effect of
the decrease in CIT, leading to a significant increase in tax evasion
for LLCs in ST-liable industries.

Proprietorships show a more heterogeneous response
(Figure~\ref{fig-fiscal-prt}, Table~\ref{tbl-fiscal-prt}). In ST-liable
industries, their overreporting barely changed in 1984--1985, consistent
with the income-tax cut compensating them for much of the sales-tax
increase (Section~\ref{sec-reform}); it rose from 1986 onward, peaking
in 1987--1988. In ST-exempt industries, their overreporting rose after
1983, even though they faced only the income-tax cut. This result is
unexpected, and I offer two possible explanations. First, the incentive
to overreport depends on the marginal tax rate, not the average one, and
the 1983 cut in marginal rates was much smaller than the cut in average
rates at both ends of the income distribution: close to zero at low
incomes and about 2 percentage points at high incomes
(Table~\ref{tbl-marg-tax-1983}). Second, the 1982--1983 changes stopped
individuals from fully deducting their financial expenses (Ocampo and
Perry 1983), which gave proprietorships, but not LLCs, a reason to
replace a lost deduction with overreported inputs.

Proprietorships' standard errors are also several times those of LLCs,
which suggests that they responded less uniformly. This is plausible:
proprietorships range from family workshops to young entrepreneurial
firms, with varying degrees of process standardization, record-keeping
and attitudes towards risk, while LLCs tend to be larger firms with more
established processes, and their responses look more homogeneous.

\chapter{The Counterfactual: Claimed Deductions and the Tax
Rate}\label{sec-counterfactual}

\section{Partial Equilibrium Claimed-Deductions
Curve}\label{sec-cf-question}

How do tax revenues change as the government changes the sales tax rate?
As the tax rate increases, the incentives to claim additional fictitious
deductions increase, but an increase in evasion raises the probability
of detection. Therefore, firms face a trade-off. On the flip side, a
decrease in the tax rate reduces the incentives to evade and firms start
to decrease the level of overreporting (intensive margin) and some firms
start jumping out of evasion altogether (extensive margin). For the
government, taking the current observed state as given, what is the
marginal change in revenue as the tax rate changes?

I study this question using the estimates of the structural objects from
the firms' tax evasion model. With the parameters at hand, I construct a
partial equilibrium claims-only Laffer curve. The counterfactual curve
allows me to investigate how the government's tax revenue changes as the
sales tax rate changes.

The results show a claims curve with a timid slope on the tax-cut side
that accelerates quickly on the tax-increase side. I find that revenue
losses due to reductions in claims would not significantly fall from
baseline unless there is a tax cut of at least 8\%. In contrast, an
increase of 0.5\% will significantly increase inflated claims.

Given the firm's optimization problem, the government's expected tax
revenue is,

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-govt-rev}{
R_i(\Delta;M_i) = \tilde\tau_{it}\big(P_tY_{it} - \big[M_i+(1-q(e'_i(\Delta)))e'_i(\Delta)\big]\big)
}\end{equation}

where \(\tilde\tau_{it}\equiv(1+\Delta)\tau_{it}\). The first term in
Equation~\ref{eq-govt-rev} is the revenue collected from sales taxes on
sales, \(\tilde\tau_{it}P_tY_{it}\). The second term is the credits
claimed on sales taxes on purchases. The term includes the firms' true
inputs, \(M_i\), plus the counterfactual undetected evasion,
\((1-q(e'_i(\Delta)))e'_i(\Delta)\), at tax rate percentage change
\(\Delta\). The firm's FOC shows \(\tau\) directly modifies the marginal
benefit of evasion leading to changes in the optimal evasion
\(e'_i(\Delta)\).

In general, governments can also choose the level of enforcement effort,
and therefore they can affect \(q(\cdot)\). In addition, governments
might collect additional income by penalizing firms caught evading.

Since I lack information on these margins, I restrict my attention to
marginal changes in tax revenue losses through changes in expected
claims as the tax rate changes. In other words, I fix output and optimal
input decisions, and focus only on

\[
-\tilde\tau_{it}\big[M_i+(1-q(e'_i(\Delta)))e'_i(\Delta)\big]
\]

\section{Estimating the model}\label{estimating-the-model}

\subsection{Setup}\label{setup-1}

Using estimates of the production function parameters
\((\beta,\alpha_K, \alpha_L)\) and constructed residuals,
\(\mathcal V_{it}\), \(\tilde{\mathcal W}_{it}\) from
Chapter~\ref{sec-deconvolution} and Chapter~\ref{sec-pf}, the objective
is to recover the parameters of the probability of detection and the
cost of evasion function \((q(e),\kappa(e,\omega))\).

As described in Chapter~\ref{sec-model}, I follow the classical tax
evasion literature building on the seminal work of Allingham and Sandmo
(1972). In this literature, the probability of detection, \(q(e)\), is
increasing in the evasion level. This assumption is consistent with a
tax authority that audits a firm whenever its overreporting exceeds a
random threshold.\footnote{The probability of detection might also
  depend on enforcement effort \(a\), \(q(e,a)\). However, since I do
  not observe enforcement, I treat it as constant across firms and time.}
The cost of evasion, \(\kappa_{it}(e,\omega)\), represents the resources
of the firm allocated to the evasion endeavour that could have been
employed in a productive activity for the firm. The cost of evasion is
increasing in the evasion level and convex in productivity. The \(\psi\)
term, an exogenous cost shock, allows cross-firm heterogeneity.

The cost of evasion, convex in productivity, rationalizes the observed
pattern in Carrillo et al. (2022), where evasion is increasing along the
size distribution of firms but drops drastically at the very top. In the
production function literature, firms are typically assumed to share a
common technology and differ only in their productivity, and thus
productivity represents a major component of size. In the model, two
primitives govern firms' evasion decisions: the probability of detection
and the cost of evasion. The probability of detection cannot explain the
increase in evasion along the size distribution. Holding the cost of
evasion constant, the probability of detection would have to decrease as
firms grow in size, meaning bigger firms are less likely to be audited,
which does not make economic sense. If anything, the probability of
detection could remain flat along the size distribution for all but the
very largest firms; higher scrutiny of the very largest firms could add
to the drop at the top, but it cannot produce the rise below it.

In contrast, the pattern can be rationalized by the cost of evasion
being an economic opportunity cost. Take, for example, the manager's
time. For small firms, managers commonly multitask across different
productive activities, such as sales, HR, purchasing, and administrative
tasks. For these firms, allocating time to tax evasion is costly. As
firms increase in size and resources, managers delegate activities
according to the firm's division of labour. For large firms, allocating
some of the manager's time to evasion is not as costly. However, as
firms grow past a certain threshold, departments grow in resources,
including the number of employees. Allocating resources to any single
activity requires coordination among a larger number of employees in
different departments. For example, the records from the warehouse,
accounting, purchasing, and finance must match for every fake invoice
used to claim deductions. Therefore, the cost of evasion might decrease
as firms get bigger but increase past a certain threshold.

Linear detection-probability and evasion-cost functions guarantee a
unique interior solution for the evasion level (see
Appendix~\ref{sec-app-soc-model}). In particular, I assume these
functions to be,

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-prob-detect}{
q(e)=\min\{\lambda e,1\}
}\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-eva-cost}{
\kappa_{it}(e_{it}, \omega_{it})=e_{it}\textbf{e}^{\delta_0-\delta_1\omega_{it}+\delta_2\omega^2_{it}+\psi_{it}}
}\end{equation}

Using the functional forms defined above, the first-order condition with
respect to tax evasion for evaders (interior solution) becomes,

\[
\tau\rho_t(1-2\lambda e_{it}) = \textbf{e}^{\delta_0-\delta_1\omega_{it}+\delta_2\omega^2_{it}+\psi_{it}}
\]

Let \[
h(e_{it};\lambda)\;\equiv\;\ln(\tau\rho_t)+\ln(1-2\lambda e_{it}),
\qquad e_{it}\in(0,\frac{1}{2\lambda}),
\]

Taking logs, the evasion FOC becomes,

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-foc-psi}{
\psi_{it}\;=\;h(e_{it};\lambda)-\delta_0+\delta_1\omega_{it}-\delta_2\omega^2_{it}
}\end{equation}

The parameters of interest are
\(\theta=(\lambda,\delta_0,\delta_1,\delta_2)\).

\subsection{Estimation}\label{sec-counter-estimation}

Using the firm-level constructed variables from
Chapter~\ref{sec-deconvolution} and Chapter~\ref{sec-pf}, I cast the
estimation problem in terms of a single unobservable, true materials
\(M_{it}\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
e(M)=M^*_{it}-M,\qquad 
\varepsilon(M)=\ln(M^*_{it}/M)-\mathcal V_{it},\qquad
\omega(M)=\tilde{\mathcal W}_{it}-(1-\beta)\varepsilon(M)
\end{aligned}
\]

Therefore, I rewrite Equation~\ref{eq-foc-psi} as,

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-psi-of-m}{
\psi(M)\;=\;h\big(e(M)\big)-\delta_0+\delta_1\omega(M)-\delta_2\omega(M)^2
}\end{equation}

In addition to depending on a single unobservable, \(M_{it}\), the setup
presents two non-standard features. First, it is nonlinear and
nonseparable in \(M\) through the log term in \(h\), and thus a
traditional method of moments cannot help (one cannot interact with an
instrument to make the undesired error term go away). Second, the domain
of \(M\) depends on the parameter being estimated. The support of the
latent variable depends on \(\lambda\) because the FOC establishes the
ceiling \(e<\tfrac 1{2\lambda}\) and restricts true materials to
\(M\in(M^*_{it}-\tfrac 1{2\lambda}, M^*_{it})\).

The method \emph{Entropic Latent Variable Integration via Simulation}
(ELVIS) (Schennach 2014) is built for exactly this combination of
features.

Let \(f_Z\) denote the density of the observed data \(Z_{it}\), and let
\(f_{M|Z}\) denote a candidate conditional density of \(M_{it}\) given
\(Z_{it}\), so that \(f_{M|Z}f_Z\) is the implied joint density of
\((M_{it},Z_{it})\).\footnote{Schennach (2014) states the result for
  general probability measures. I write it with densities because, in
  this application, \(M_{it}\) is continuous on the known support
  \(\big(\max\{0,M^*_{it}-\tfrac{1}{2\lambda}\},M^*_{it}\big)\); every
  candidate conditional distribution therefore has a density there. The
  reference density \(f^0_{M|Z}\) used in estimation is the uniform
  density on that support: the estimator draws \(M_{it}\) uniformly on
  the support and accepts each draw with probability
  \(\min\{1,\exp[\gamma'(g_{\text{new}}-g_{\text{old}})]\}\); the
  chain's average therefore computes \(\tilde g\) in
  Equation~\ref{eq-elvis-tilt}.} The question is whether some choice of
\(f_{M|Z}\) makes \(E_{f_{M|Z}f_Z}[g(M,Z,\theta)]=0\).

Schennach (2014) shows the abstract \emph{existence} problem of
\(f_{M|Z}\) is equivalent to a more concrete optimization problem (her
Theorem 2.1):

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-elvis-equiv}{
\inf_{f_{M|Z}}\big\|E_{f_{M|Z}f_Z}[g(M,Z,\theta)]\big\|=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \inf_{\gamma\in\mathbb R^{d_g}}\big\|E_{f_Z}[\tilde g(Z,\theta,\gamma)]\big\|=0
}\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-elvis-tilt}{
\tilde g(Z,\theta,\gamma)\equiv\frac{\int g(M,Z,\theta)\exp\big(\gamma'g(M,Z,\theta)\big)\,f^0_{M|Z}(M|Z)\,dM}{\int \exp\big(\gamma'g(M,Z,\theta)\big)\,f^0_{M|Z}(M|Z)\,dM}
}\end{equation}

Among the conditional distributions of \(M\) given \(Z\) that satisfy
the moment conditions, ELVIS selects the one with maximum entropy
relative to an arbitrary reference density \(f^0_{M|Z}\). In the
optimization problem, \(\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{d_g}\) is the Lagrange
multiplier vector on the moment constraints. \(f^0_{M|Z}\) only needs to
be positive wherever the true conditional density of \(M\) is; any
choice with the right support gives the same \(\hat\theta\) (Remark 2.3,
Schennach 2014). In this setting, this allows me to avoid specifying
\(f_\psi\) or \(f_\varepsilon\) or searching over the space of all
conditional distributions of \(M\). Instead, I solve a finite
optimization problem.

The true (unobserved) conditional distribution of \(M\) satisfies the
moment condition at the true \(\theta_0\), so Theorem 2.1 guarantees
some \(\gamma\) also solves the right-hand side of
Equation~\ref{eq-elvis-equiv} at \(\theta_0\). Searching over \(\theta\)
for the value where this finite-dimensional problem has a solution
therefore recovers \(\theta_0\), regardless of the chosen \(f^0_{M|Z}\),
without requiring knowledge of the true conditional distribution of
\(M\).

The entropy-maximizing distribution satisfies \(E[g(M,Z,\theta_0)]=0\),
but it should not be treated as the actual distribution of \(M\), since
many conditional distributions of \(M\) given \(Z\) satisfy the moment
condition (Schennach 2022, 1250). This matters for the counterfactual
question. Since expected counterfactual revenue depends on the latent
\(M_{it}\), it would be tempting to average it over draws from such a
distribution. Instead, I include the counterfactual object as an
auxiliary parameter with its own moment condition and estimate it
jointly with \(\theta\) and \(\gamma\).

During estimation, I use the model's independence assumptions:
\(\varepsilon\) is independent of \((M,e,\omega)\) and \(\psi\) is
independent of \((\omega,M)\), with \(E[\varepsilon]=E[\psi]=0\). They
imply the following set of moments,

\[
g(M,Z,\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}
\psi(M,Z) \\
\varepsilon(M,Z) \\
\psi(M,Z)\ln M \\
\psi(M,Z)\omega(M,Z) \\
\psi(M,Z)\omega(M,Z)^2 \\
\varepsilon(M,Z)\ln M \\
\varepsilon(M,Z) e(M,Z) \\
\varepsilon(M,Z)\omega(M,Z) \\
\frac{\partial h(e,\lambda)}{\partial\lambda}\,\varepsilon(M,Z)
\end{bmatrix}
\]

The nine moments are combined into a single test statistic through the
continuously updated estimator (CUE) objective \(\hat L_n(\theta)\) of
Schennach (2014)'s Definition F.1. Her Theorem F.1 shows that, at any
\(\theta\) satisfying the moment condition,
\(TS(\theta)=2n\hat L_n(\theta)\) is asymptotically bounded above by a
\(\chi^2_{d_g}\) random variable. Comparing \(TS(\theta)\) with the
\(\chi^2_{d_g}\) critical value therefore gives a conservative test,
whose size is at most its nominal level; I call it the conservative test
throughout and report as confidence sets the tested points it does not
reject. For the nine moments, the 95\% critical value is
\(\chi^2_{9,.95}=16.92\). The exact objective and my sign convention
relative to hers are in Appendix~\ref{sec-app-elvis}.

The estimation sample has 32,232 unincorporated firm-periods (3,340 at
the \(\tau=0\) corner).

\subsection{Discussion}\label{discussion}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\(\hat\lambda\) is very small}: a few aggressive evaders
  dominate the estimate; the model is trying to fit a straight line over
  a highly right-skewed distribution.

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Mechanically, selecting a functional form for the probability of
    detection imposes a ceiling probability
  \item
    Assuming all unincorporated firms with a non-zero sales tax rate on
    purchases evade taxes implies that all firms evade below the ceiling
    (normalization)
  \item
    Alternatively, I could

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      trim the sample more (currently 0.5\%, see
      Appendix~\ref{sec-app-elvis} for details) to reduce the influence
      of aggressive evaders
    \item
      impose a normalization (e.g.~prob for 99th evaders=50\%)
    \item
      use a non-linear prob of detection to smooth the skewness of the
      evasion distribution
    \end{itemize}
  \item
    Could \(\delta_0\) and \(\lambda\) be competing to identify the
    level of evasion?

    \begin{itemize}
    \tightlist
    \item
      the estimates suggest \(\lambda\) is truly different from zero
      even though \(\delta_0\) has been profiled in all grid searches
    \item
      \(\lambda\) is identified with the score moment,
      \(E[\partial h/\partial \lambda \cdot \varepsilon]=0\) and
      \(\delta_0\) from \(E[\psi]=0\).
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{(\(\hat\delta_1, \hat\delta_2\))}: seem to be consistently
  identified by the curvature of the evasion distribution and

  searches and under the correct moments they always come up with
  correct signs
\end{itemize}

\section{Counterfactual design}\label{sec-cf-design}

Let the counterfactual tax rate be
\(\tilde\tau_{it}=(1+\Delta)\tau_{it}\). Then, given the parameter
estimates, the counterfactual evasion is,

\[
e'_i(\Delta)=\max\left(0,\ \frac{\tilde\tau_{it}\rho_t-C(\omega_i,\psi_i)}{2\lambda\,\tilde\tau_{it}\rho_t}\right),\qquad C(\omega,\psi)\equiv\textbf{e}^{\delta_0-\delta_1\omega+\delta_2\omega^2+\psi}
\]

Firms might jump out of evasion and optimally select \(e'_i=0\) as
\(\Delta\) adjusts the marginal benefit of evasion. This decision
justifies \(\max(0,\cdot)\).

Substituting the baseline FOC, \(e'_i\) can be written in terms of the
firm's baseline evasion \(e_i\),

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-counter-e}{
e'_i(\Delta)=\max\!\big(0,\frac{1}{1+\Delta}[e_i+\Delta/(2\lambda)]\big)
}\end{equation}

where \(e_i=M^*_i-M_i\) is the firm's baseline evasion at the current
state, a function of the latent \(M_i\), and captures the firm
heterogeneity defined by \((\omega, \psi)\). Equation~\ref{eq-counter-e}
shows that the counterfactual evasion is linear in the model's current
evasion with slope \(1/(1+\Delta)\) and an intercept,
\(\Delta/[2\lambda(1+\Delta)]\), constant across firms.

The counterfactual evasion is a function of the same latent \(M_i\) as
the main moments system. As briefly mentioned in
Section~\ref{sec-counter-estimation}, Define firm \(i\)'s counterfactual
deduction claims as
\(\text{Claims}_i(\Delta)\equiv\tilde\tau_{it}\big[M_i+(1-q(e'_i(\Delta)))e'_i(\Delta)\big]\),
the credit claimed on true inputs plus undetected overreporting. I
include an auxiliary parameter \(\theta_C(\Delta)\), mean deduction
claims, with its own moment
\(E\big[\text{Claims}_i(\Delta)-\theta_C(\Delta)\big]=0\). Claims are
measured in nominal COP; each firm-period's claims are deflated by the
GDP deflator of its year before averaging, so \(\theta_C(\Delta)\) is
real mean claims per firm-period. Therefore, the number of moments
increases to \(d_g=10\).

To obtain the confidence set of \(\theta_C(\Delta)\), I invert the test.
At each \(\Delta\), \(\theta\) is fixed at the best point from
Section~\ref{sec-cf-estimates} and only \(\gamma\) is re-optimized
(Aguiar and Kashaev 2020). I grid over candidate \(\theta_C\) and build
the confidence set
\(\{\theta_C: TS(\Delta,\theta_C)<\chi^2_{10,.95}=18.31\}\).

\section{Estimation mechanics}\label{estimation-mechanics}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  CUE quadratic objective with eigenvalue-truncated weighting matrix.
\item
  \textbf{Sales-tax revenue on sales is observed.} The data record the
  sales tax each firm pays on its sales in each period, which is the
  observed counterpart of \(\tau_{it}P_tY_{it}\) in
  Equation~\ref{eq-govt-rev}; total revenue in the counterfactual is
  computed from it directly, held at its baseline value.
\item
  \textbf{The \(\lambda\)-score moment enters in bounded form.} The
  score
  \(h_\lambda(e)\equiv\partial h(e;\lambda)/\partial\lambda=-2e/(1-2\lambda e)\)
  diverges near the FOC ceiling \(e\to1/(2\lambda)\), where a single
  extreme draw can dominate the weighting matrix's eigenvalues and
  silently collapse the effective moment count. The implementation
  therefore replaces \(h_\lambda\) with
  \(h_\lambda/(1-h_\lambda)\in(-1,0]\) in the ninth moment
  (\(1-2\lambda e\) floored at \(10^{-6}\)). Validity is unaffected: any
  function of \(e\) alone is independent of \(\varepsilon\) under the
  same argument that licenses the raw score. The transform decays
  polynomially, not exponentially, so firms stay numerically
  distinguishable across the whole range of \(e\); \(\exp(h_\lambda)\)
  and \(\tanh(h_\lambda)\) were rejected because both saturate to a
  constant for most of the sample.
\item
  Per-firm Metropolis chain: \(n_{burn}=1000\), \(n_{keep}=3000\)
  (settled convention -- see CLAUDE.md's ``MCMC chain-length
  convention'' entry for why this length, not a longer one, and why
  acceptance-rate SD is the more reliable diagnostic than \(\gamma'g\)
  variance).
\item
  Nelder-Mead optimizer (beats BOBYQA decisively on this problem -- see
  CLAUDE.md).
\item
  \(\gamma\) is unbounded in the optimizer (Schennach and AK2020 both
  treat \(\gamma\to\pm\infty\) as an expected solution, not a numerical
  failure).
\end{itemize}

\section{Test inversion: the conservative test, and
why}\label{test-inversion-the-conservative-test-and-why}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Source definition (Schennach 2014, Definition F.1 and Theorem
  F.1):} with
  \(\hat g(\theta,\gamma)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\tilde g(Z_i,\theta,\gamma)\)
  (the sample average of the tilted moment from
  Equation~\ref{eq-elvis-tilt}) and
  \(\hat V(\theta,\gamma)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\tilde g(Z_i,\theta,\gamma)\tilde g(Z_i,\theta,\gamma)'\),
  her continuous updating objective is
  \(\hat L_n^S(\theta)=\sup_{\gamma\in\mathbb R^{d_g}}-\tfrac12\,\hat g(\theta,\gamma)'\hat V(\theta,\gamma)^{-}\hat g(\theta,\gamma)\),
  with \(\hat V^-\) a generalized inverse. Theorem F.1: at any
  \(\theta\) satisfying the moment condition, \(-2n\hat L_n^S(\theta)\)
  is asymptotically bounded above by \(\chi^2_{d_g}\). Maximizing
  \(\hat L_n^S\) over \(\gamma\) is the same as minimizing the
  nonnegative quadratic form \(\tfrac12\hat g'\hat V^-\hat g\); we
  report this positive quantity as \(\hat L_n(\theta)\), so
  \(TS(\theta)=2n\hat L_n(\theta)=-2n\hat L_n^S(\theta)\) --- same
  statistic, sign flipped purely so the optimizer minimizes a
  nonnegative objective instead of maximizing a nonpositive one.
  \(\hat V^-\) in our implementation discards eigen-directions with
  eigenvalues below \(10^{-8}\) times the largest, for numerical
  stability.
\item
  \textbf{Conservative test} (Theorem F.1, absolute):
  \(TS_{\text{cons}}=2n\hat L_n\) against \(\chi^2_{d_g,.95}\) directly
  -- no credit for profiling out nuisance parameters or \(\gamma\).
\item
  \textbf{Why the conservative test:} the counterfactual's purpose is to
  support a policy claim, so the confidence sets use the test whose size
  is guaranteed not to exceed its nominal level. \textless!-- DROPPED
  2026-09-23 (Hans): the soft (min-subtracted) test is not used in the
  thesis.
\item
  \textbf{Two test statistics, same \(\hat L_n\), different critical
  value:}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \textbf{Conservative test} (Theorem F.1, absolute):
    \(TS_{\text{cons}}=2n\hat L_n\) against \(\chi^2_{d_g,.95}\)
    directly -- no credit for profiling out nuisance parameters or
    \(\gamma\).
  \item
    \textbf{Soft test} (min-subtracted, CHT-style):
    \(TS_{\text{soft}}=2n[\hat L_n-\min\hat L_n]\) against the same
    \(\chi^2_{d_g,.95}\) -- profiling gives back some degrees of freedom
    informally, so this is strictly more powerful (rejects more), and
    \(TS_{\text{cons}}=TS_{\text{soft}}+2n\hat L_{n,\min}\) exactly, a
    fixed additive shift. The conservative-passing region is therefore
    always nested inside the soft-passing region.
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{Why the conservative test is reported as headline here} (a
  real, considered choice, not the only one used in this project -- the
  stage-2 \((\lambda,\delta_1,\delta_2)\) estimates elsewhere use the
  soft test as operative): once genuine conservative-test-passing points
  existed for the first time in this project's history (the 3D cube
  result, Section~\ref{sec-cf-estimates}), the more conservative test
  became the defensible one to lead with for a counterfactual whose
  whole purpose is to make a policy claim -- the soft test remains
  reported alongside it, not discarded. --\textgreater{}
\item
  \textbf{Why test inversion over a bootstrap CI:} an earlier attempt
  (plant-block bootstrap on the PF-step elasticities, a different part
  of this project) gave bias-corrected percentile intervals that crossed
  outside the parameter's own natural domain (\([0,1]\) for an
  elasticity) -- an artifact of the CI formula, not of the estimator.
  Test inversion only ever evaluates points on the bounded grid actually
  tested, so this can't happen; the ``cost'' is a set-valued answer
  instead of a point \(\pm\) SE, treated in this project as an honest
  feature, not a fallback (Schennach herself: adding moments shrinks the
  identified set, ``not a fallback to avoid'').
\item
  \textbf{AK2020 precedent, followed directly, not just cited:} Aguiar
  and Kashaev (2020)'s own counterfactual appendix
  (\texttt{Appendix\_F/F\_figure1.R}) uses exactly this design -- grid a
  candidate outcome, profile the nuisance parameters (their \(\gamma\))
  at each cell, plot pass/fail by candidate, connect the passing
  region's own upper/lower bounds across the grid. The bound-plot
  convention used for every \((\Delta,R)\) figure in this chapter (grey
  \(\times\) = rejected, coloured \(\bullet\) = passes, dashed/dotted
  bound lines) is a direct visual analogue of their own figure, not an
  independent design.
\item
  Full derivations: Schennach (2014), Theorem F.1 and its proof;
  \texttt{Paper/sections/9999-elvis.qmd}.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Second-Order Condition: Single-Tax
Model}\label{sec-app-soc-model}

This appendix derives the second-order condition (SOC) for the firm's
problem in Chapter~\ref{sec-model}, and shows how it translates into the
curvature restriction on the detection function \(q(e)\) used throughout
the paper.

\section{Setup}\label{setup-2}

Recall the firm's problem, Equation~\ref{eq-profit}, and its two
first-order conditions, Equation~\ref{eq-foc-m} and
Equation~\ref{eq-foc-e}. For a candidate solution
\((K_{it}^*,L_{it}^*,M_{it}^*,e_{it}^*)\) to be a maximum, the Hessian
of expected profit must be negative semi-definite at that point.

\section{The Hessian is
block-diagonal}\label{the-hessian-is-block-diagonal}

Two features of the model make the Hessian easy to sign.

First, \(M_{it}\perp e_{it}\): neither the materials FOC
(Equation~\ref{eq-foc-m}) nor the evasion FOC (Equation~\ref{eq-foc-e})
contains the other's choice variable, so every cross-partial between
\(e_{it}\) and \((K_{it},L_{it},M_{it})\) is zero. Second, the
production function
\(Y_{it}=K_{it}^{\alpha_K}L_{it}^{\alpha_L}M_{it}^\beta\exp(\omega_{it}+\varepsilon_{it})\)
is Cobb-Douglas, hence strictly concave in \((K_{it},L_{it},M_{it})\)
jointly. The Hessian is therefore block-diagonal,
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-app-soc-hessian}{
\nabla^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial e_{it}^2} & 0 & 0 & 0\\[4pt]
0 & \dfrac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial M_{it}^2} & \dfrac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial M_{it}\partial L_{it}} & \dfrac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial M_{it}\partial K_{it}}\\[4pt]
0 & \dfrac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial L_{it}\partial M_{it}} & \dfrac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial L_{it}^2} & \dfrac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial L_{it}\partial K_{it}}\\[4pt]
0 & \dfrac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial K_{it}\partial M_{it}} & \dfrac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial K_{it}\partial L_{it}} & \dfrac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial K_{it}^2}
\end{bmatrix}
}\end{equation}

with a single nonzero entry in the first row and column. Negative
definiteness requires the leading principal minors to alternate in sign
(odd-order minors negative, even-order positive). Since the lower
\(3\times3\) block is already negative definite (strict concavity of the
Cobb-Douglas production function in \(K_{it},L_{it},M_{it}\)), the
\emph{only} condition the evasion margin adds to the firm's problem is
that the first leading principal minor --- the top-left scalar entry ---
is itself negative: \[
\frac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial e_{it}^2}<0.
\]

The capital, labour, and materials choices place no restriction on the
evasion technology, and vice versa; the SOC for \(e_{it}\) can be
analyzed entirely on its own.

\section{The scalar condition}\label{the-scalar-condition}

Differentiating the evasion FOC (Equation~\ref{eq-foc-e}) once more with
respect to \(e_{it}\),
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-app-soc-full}{
\frac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial e_{it}^2}=-\tau\rho_t(1+\phi)\Big(2q'(e_{it})+e_{it}q''(e_{it})\Big)-\frac{\partial^2\kappa_{it}(e_{it},\omega_{it})}{\partial e_{it}^2}<0.
}\end{equation}

Rearranging, the SOC holds whenever \[
\tau\rho_t(1+\phi)\Big(2q'(e_{it})+e_{it}q''(e_{it})\Big)+\frac{\partial^2\kappa_{it}(e_{it},\omega_{it})}{\partial e_{it}^2}>0,
\] a sum of two terms. It is therefore \emph{sufficient} --- not
necessary --- that either term alone be strictly positive:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-app-soc-split}{
\frac{\partial^2\kappa_{it}(e_{it},\omega_{it})}{\partial e_{it}^2}\ge0
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
\tau\rho_t(1+\phi)\Big(2q'(e_{it})+e_{it}q''(e_{it})\Big)\ge0.
}\end{equation}

\textbf{Under this paper's headline cost function},
\(\kappa_{it}(e_{it},\omega_{it})=e_{it}\exp\{\delta_0-\delta_1\omega_{it}+\delta_2\omega_{it}^2+\psi_{it}\}\)
--- linear in \(e_{it}\) --- the first term is identically zero,
\(\partial^2\kappa_{it}/\partial e_{it}^2=0\). The cost function
therefore contributes nothing toward satisfying the SOC on its own; the
entire burden falls on the second condition, the curvature of the
detection function.

\section{The curvature restriction}\label{the-curvature-restriction}

Since \(\tau\rho_t(1+\phi)>0\), the second condition in
Equation~\ref{eq-app-soc-split} reduces to
\(2q'(e_{it})+e_{it}q''(e_{it})\ge0\). Economic theory imposes
\(q'(e_{it})>0\) (detection is increasing in overreporting) and
\(e_{it}>0\) for an interior evader, so dividing through by
\(q'(e_{it})e_{it}>0\) gives
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-app-soc-final}{
-\frac{q''(e_{it})}{q'(e_{it})}\,e_{it}<2.
}\end{equation}

This is a restriction on the \textbf{curvature of \(q\) alone} --- a
firm's evasion cost never enters, given the linear-in-\(e\)
specification above. It says \(q'\) may decline as \(e_{it}\) rises (a
concave detection function), but not too fast relative to its own level:
the semi-elasticity of the marginal detection probability with respect
to \(e_{it}\) must exceed \(-2\).

\textbf{Linear detection, \(q(e)=\min\{\lambda e,1\}\)} (headline
specification): \(q''(e)=0\) on the interior, so \(-q''e/q'=0<2\)
unconditionally --- the SOC holds at every interior point, with no
restriction on \(\lambda e\) beyond the FOC's own ceiling
(\(\lambda e<1/2\), from Chapter~\ref{sec-model}).

\textbf{Concave detection, \(q(e)=1-e^{-\lambda e}\)}:
\(q'(e)=\lambda e^{-\lambda e}\) and
\(q''(e)=-\lambda^2e^{-\lambda e}\), so \(-q''e/q'=\lambda e\). The SOC
requires \(\lambda e<2\) --- a genuine restriction, distinct from (and,
for this functional form, looser than) the FOC's own ceiling
\(\lambda e<1\) derived in Chapter~\ref{sec-model}. Since
\(\lambda e<1\implies\lambda e<2\), the FOC ceiling is the binding
constraint in practice; the SOC is automatically satisfied wherever the
FOC has an interior solution at all.

\textbf{Convex detection, \(q(e)=\min\{(\lambda e)^k,1\}\), \(k>1\)}:
\(q'(e)=k\lambda(\lambda e)^{k-1}\) and
\(q''(e)=k(k-1)\lambda^2(\lambda e)^{k-2}\), so \(-q''e/q'=1-k\). Since
\(k>1\), this is always negative, hence always less than \(2\) ---
convex detection satisfies the SOC unconditionally, for any \(k>1\).

\textbf{Summary.} All three candidate detection functions satisfy the
second-order condition on the relevant part of their domain --- linear
and convex unconditionally, concave provided \(\lambda e<2\), which is
already implied by that function's own FOC-ceiling. The choice among
them is therefore not disciplined by the SOC; it is a modelling choice
about the detection technology, addressed on economic grounds in
Chapter~\ref{sec-model}.
