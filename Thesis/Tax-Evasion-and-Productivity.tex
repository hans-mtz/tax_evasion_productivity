\chapter*{Abstract}\label{abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

\markboth{Abstract}{Abstract}

Sales and value-added taxes that credit purchases reward firms for
overreporting their inputs: every fictitious dollar of deductible inputs
lowers their tax liability, and the higher the rate, the larger the
reward. How this evasion responds to the tax rate is largely unknown,
because true inputs are not observed and firms that overreport may be
mistaken for low-productivity firms. This thesis separates overreporting
from productivity using the production function, with corporations,
which government and market scrutiny keep from evading, as the
truth-reporting benchmark. The approach needs only reported inputs and
output, so it applies equally to standard firm-level surveys and
administrative data; I apply it to Colombian manufacturing firms from
1981 to 1991. First, a statistical test, valid for any detection
probability, evasion cost and common technology, and whose rejections
can only point to overreporting, detects overreporting in 9 of 20
industries, including four of the five largest. Second, deconvolution
recovers its distribution: in the seven industries where the test
rejects at the 1\% level, firms overreport on average 16\% of their true
materials in the median industry. Third, correcting for overreporting
yields consistently smaller output elasticities of materials and less
dispersed, more persistent productivity, with a 90--10 ratio about 40\%
lower and a half-life of productivity shocks 1.6 to 3.1 times longer.
Fourth, after a 1983 reform raised the sales-tax rate in most
industries, overreporting by unincorporated firms in the affected
industries rose by about 9 percentage points of true materials between
1983 and 1987. Finally, a structural model of detection and evasion
costs shows that a 0.5\% increase in the sales-tax rate raises claimed
deductions by between 16\% and 28\%, far more than its mechanical
effect, while cuts lower them only gradually: the smallest cut
statistically distinguishable from current policy is 8\%.

\chapter{Introduction}\label{sec-intro}

Sales and value-added taxes that credit purchases reward firms for
overreporting their inputs: every fictitious dollar of deductible inputs
lowers the tax a firm owes, and the reward grows with the tax rate.
Firms act on this incentive. Tax authorities around the world document
input overreporting, often through invoices for purchases that never
took place (OECD 2017), and the losses they have measured are large:
between 1.2\% and 2.7\% of total tax revenue in Chile, Colombia,
Ecuador, Poland and Slovakia, and about a ninth of the entire VAT
compliance gap in Colombia and Slovakia (OECD 2017; Carrillo et al.
2022; CASE and IHS 2018; Longinotti 2022). Because these figures count
only what enforcement detects, true losses are plausibly larger. The
gains also accrue at the top: in Ecuador, the tax evaded by inflating
costs, as a share of owners' income, is 56 times higher for owners in
the top 5\% of the income distribution than for those in the bottom 80\%
(Carrillo et al. 2022).

How individuals' reported income responds to tax rates is well
documented; how firms' overreporting of inputs responds is not. For
individuals, a large literature estimates how reported income responds
to the tax rate and shows that the response includes evasion and
avoidance as well as changes in real activity (Saez et al. 2012; Chetty
2009). For firms, evidence on responses to tax rates comes mostly from
bunching at kinks and notches in the tax schedule (Best et al. 2015) and
from misreported imports (Fisman and Wei 2004). Moreover, economic
theory suggests that optimal commodity-tax rates should be lower on
goods whose evasion is more elastic with respect to the tax rate (Cremer
and Gahvari 1993). Yet, to my knowledge, no study has estimated how
firms' overreporting of inputs, a form of tax evasion that the credit on
purchases in sales and value-added taxes incentivizes, responds to the
tax rate. One reason is that overreporting is not observed in the data:
for a given level of output, a firm reporting high input use may be
overreporting, or it may suffer from low productivity.

I investigate whether input overreporting rises with the tax rate, and
the extent to which it affects government tax revenue through firms'
claimed deductions. I find that, after Colombia raised its sales-tax
rate in 1983 for most manufacturing industries, while exempt industries
remained exempt, overreporting by unincorporated firms in the industries
affected by the tax increase rose significantly, by about 9 percentage
points of true materials between 1983 and 1987. In a structural model
estimated on all industries, I find that a 0.5\% increase in the
sales-tax rate raises claimed deductions by 16\% to 28\%, far more than
the 0.5\% that a naive proportional calculation would suggest, while
cuts lower claimed deductions only gradually: the smallest cut
distinguishable from current policy is 8\%. A back-of-the-envelope
calculation suggests that undetected overreporting costs about 3\% of
potential net sales-tax revenue from unincorporated firms in the sample.
Near the current rate, and holding the tax collected on sales fixed, the
elasticity of this revenue with respect to the tax rate is about −20: a
1\% increase in the rate lowers it by about 20\%
(Section~\ref{sec-app-backofenvelope}).

To distinguish between evasion and productivity, I employ a structural
production-function approach, and then use it to study how evasion
responds to the tax rate. In the absence of evasion, the first-order
condition for materials, the flexible deductible input, reveals the
structural relationship between the firm's materials share of revenue
and the common technology within the industry. Corporations are closely
monitored by the government and face the market's reaction to tax
aggressiveness, which keeps them from overreporting; their reports allow
me to identify that technology in each industry. Among unincorporated
firms, deviations from the common technology reveal overreporting up to
measurement error in output. Deconvolution then separates the
distribution of overreporting from that of the measurement error, and
the production-function estimates recover productivity net of
overreporting. To study how overreporting responds to the tax rate, I
exploit the 1983 reform, measuring overreporting against the output
elasticity of materials identified from corporations. Finally, employing
an entropy-based method that does not require specifying the
distribution of the unobservables (Schennach 2014), I estimate a
structural model in which firms weigh the tax saved on overreported
inputs against the probability of detection and a cost of evasion that
varies with productivity.

Separating overreporting from productivity also yields three findings of
its own. First, a simple test compares unincorporated firms' materials
shares with the corporate benchmark in each industry. It rejects the
absence of overreporting in 9 of 20 manufacturing industries, including
four of the five largest, which together produce about two fifths of
manufacturing output, and it rejects decisively, at the 1\% level, in
seven of them, where unincorporated firms overreport on average 16\% of
their true materials in the median industry. The test is robust: it
remains valid under any detection probability and cost of evasion that
vanish without evasion, and under any common production technology. A
rejection is also unambiguous, because no firm gains by underreporting
deductible inputs, so it can only point to overreporting. Second,
ignoring overreporting distorts the production function and, with it,
measured productivity: in those seven industries, correcting for
overreporting recovers output elasticities of materials consistently
lower than uncorrected estimates (Gandhi et al. 2020), and less
dispersed and more persistent productivity distributions, with a ratio
of the 90th to the 10th percentile about 40\% lower on average and a
half-life of productivity shocks 1.6 to 3.1 times longer. Third, the
structural estimates show a cost of evasion that is convex in
productivity and lowest near the 86th percentile of productivity, which
rationalizes why evasion rises with firm size but drastically drops at
the very top (Carrillo et al. 2022).

These results relate to three strands of public economics and to one of
productivity measurement. First, I estimate how a particular form of
evasion by firms responds to the tax rate, a response the literature on
taxes and revenue has mostly seen through individuals' reported income.
Traditionally, the elasticity of personal taxable income has combined
all different responses to the rate, evasion included, into a single
statistic (Feldstein 1999; Slemrod and Kopczuk 2002; Saez et al. 2012).
However, when part of the cost of evasion is a transfer rather than a
resource cost, this elasticity alone no longer measures the efficiency
cost of taxation (Chetty 2009). Measuring that cost requires knowing how
evasion itself responds to the rate, separately from real activity.
Direct evidence on this response is scarcer, and comes from the gap
between households' consumption and reported income (Gorodnichenko et
al. 2009) and, for firms, from bunching at kinks in the tax schedule
(Best et al. 2015). I provide such evidence for firms' overreporting of
inputs under a sales tax that credits purchases, from both a statutory
rate change and a structural model that separates overreporting from
productivity. The structural model also provides an element that the
theory of optimal commodity taxation under evasion requires: how claimed
deductions respond to the rate (Cremer and Gahvari 1993).

Second, I provide measures of firms' cost overreporting without relying
on confidential tax records. Most evidence on firm evasion focuses on
underreported revenue (Slemrod 2019). Yet the cost side matters: firms
offset enforcement on revenue by raising reported costs (Carrillo et al.
2017; Asatryan and Peichl 2017), and paper trails make transactions
between firms harder to misreport than sales to final consumers
(Pomeranz 2015); therefore, firms that sell to other firms have little
room on sales and more on costs. Cost overreporting has mostly been
measured directly with transaction-level tax records in Ecuador and
Mexico, which reveal the mechanism: firms acquiring fake invoices from
ghost firms (Carrillo et al. 2022; Zumaya et al. 2021). Tax records,
however, are confidential, available for few countries, and accessible
only to selected researchers. Survey-based measures of firm evasion rely
instead on firms' self-reported share of sales declared to the tax
authority (Dabla-Norris et al. 2019), whose truthfulness cannot be
verified. My approach needs only reported inputs and output, and is thus
equally applicable to standard firm-level surveys and administrative
records.

Third, I extend the validation-sample approach from individuals to
firms. This approach infers evasion by comparing an indirect measure of
true income between a truth-reporting group and a group that may
misreport. For example, the self-employed report less income relative to
their food consumption than employees, whose income is reported by their
employers (Pissarides and Weber 1989). Another application finds that
about 12\% of wages and salaries in Estonia go unreported (Paulus 2015).
The same comparison has also been used to measure how evasion responds
to a tax reform (Gorodnichenko et al. 2009). For firms, however, the
natural indirect measure, output relative to inputs, also depends on an
additional unobservable, productivity: a firm reporting high input use
may be overreporting or may simply have had a negative productivity
shock. I use the production function to separate the two, with
corporations as the truth-reporting group. Unlike the comparisons for
individuals, mine does not require the two groups to be alike in size or
productivity: corporations are much larger than unincorporated firms,
and the production function accounts for the differences in productivity
behind this gap.

Finally, this thesis shows how tax-motivated misreporting of inputs
distorts production-function estimates. Proxy-variable methods rely on a
flexible input, typically materials, either inverting its demand
(Levinsohn and Petrin 2003; Ackerberg et al. 2015) or using its
first-order condition (Gandhi et al. 2020), and treat reported inputs as
true. Work on mismeasured inputs has focused on classical, mean-zero
measurement error, mostly in capital (Collard-Wexler and Loecker 2020).
Overreporting differs on both counts: it is an optimal choice that
varies with productivity, so it is neither mean zero nor independent of
productivity, and it affects exactly the input on which these methods
rely. Correcting for it reveals that the output elasticity of materials
is consistently lower than uncorrected estimates and that productivity
distributions are less dispersed and more persistent, findings that
matter for any analysis built on measured productivity differences
across firms.

The rest of the thesis follows the argument. Chapter~\ref{sec-model}
shows why a firm would overreport materials and what holds it back, and
Chapter~\ref{sec-setting} explains why Colombian corporations can serve
as truth-tellers. Chapter~\ref{sec-testing} shows that overreporting can
be detected from production data alone, Chapter~\ref{sec-deconvolution}
measures how much firms overreport, and Chapter~\ref{sec-pf} shows what
correcting for it does to production-function estimates.
Chapter~\ref{sec-fiscal} uses the 1983 reform to show that overreporting
responds to the tax rate, and Chapter~\ref{sec-counterfactual} asks how
much claimed deductions would move under other rates.
Chapter~\ref{sec-literature} relates these results to the literature,
and Chapter~\ref{sec-conclusion}, as tradition demands, concludes.

\chapter{A Model of Tax Evasion through Input
Overreporting}\label{sec-model}

\section{Setup}\label{setup}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Firm chooses \(K_{it},L_{it},M_{it}\) (true materials) and
  \(e_{it}\ge0\) (overreported materials, \(M^*_{it}=M_{it}+e_{it}\) is
  what's reported to the tax authority) to maximize expected profit.
\item
  Detection: à la Allingham and Sandmo (1972) -- tax authority audits
  with probability \(q(e_{it})\), always catches evasion if audited (no
  partial detection). \(q(e_{it})\in[0,1]\), increasing, \(q(0)=0\).
\item
  If caught, the firm repays the excess credit claimed on \(e_{it}\),
  plus a penalty multiplier \((1+\phi)\) (usually \(\phi=0\)).
\item
  Cost of evasion \(\kappa_{it}(e_{it},\omega_{it})\): resources
  (e.g.~manager's time) diverted from production to concealment.
  Increasing in \(e_{it}\); assumed independent of \(M_{it}\) (choosing
  how much to overreport doesn't enter the materials choice or vice
  versa -- \(M\perp e\mid\omega\)).
\item
  Production:
  \(Y_{it}=K_{it}^{\alpha_K}L_{it}^{\alpha_L}M_{it}^{\beta}\exp(\omega_{it}+\varepsilon_{it})\),
  \(\omega\) = productivity (state variable, AR(1) later),
  \(\varepsilon\) = measurement error in output, \(E[\varepsilon]=0\)
  (not an ex-post output shock as in GNR 2020); productivity is
  \(\exp(\omega)\), not \(\exp(\omega+\varepsilon)\).
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-profit}{
\begin{aligned}
  \max_{K,L,M,e} \; \mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}] = &(1-\tau)\big(P_t\mathbb{E}[Y_{it}]-\rho_tM_{it}\big)-r_tK_{it}-w_tL_{it}\\
  &+\big[1-q(e_{it})\big]\rho_t\tau e_{it}-q(e_{it})\phi\rho_t\tau e_{it}-\kappa_{it}(e_{it},\omega_{it})
\end{aligned}
}\end{equation}

subject to the production function above. (\(\rho_t\) = materials price,
\(\tau\) = tax rate.)

\section{First-order conditions}\label{first-order-conditions}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Materials FOC} (standard, static, doesn't involve \(e\)):
  \begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-foc-m}{
  \beta K_{it}^{\alpha_K}L_{it}^{\alpha_L}M_{it}^{-(1-\beta)}\exp(\omega_{it})=\rho_t/P_t
  }\end{equation}
\item
  \textbf{Evasion FOC} (interior solution):
  \begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-foc-e}{
  \tau\rho_t\big(1-(1+\phi)(q(e_{it})+q'(e_{it})e_{it})\big)=\partial\kappa_{it}(e_{it},\omega_{it})/\partial e_{it}
  }\end{equation}
\item
  \textbf{Corner solution:} some firms optimally choose \(e_{it}=0\) --
  marginal cost exceeds marginal benefit at every \(e>0\). For these
  firms Equation~\ref{eq-foc-e} becomes an inequality,
  \(\tau\rho_t\le\partial\kappa_{it}(0,\omega_{it})/\partial e_{it}\).

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \textbf{Corporations are constrained non-evaders}: audited
    statements / third-party reporting make \(e=0\) a hard institutional
    constraint for them, not a corner outcome of Equation~\ref{eq-foc-e}
    -- used only in the first stage (ch.~4-5), outside the evasion FOC
    entirely.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section{Second-order condition}\label{second-order-condition}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Profit's Hessian is block-diagonal (\(e\) separate from \(K,L,M\)
  since \(M\perp e\)) and the production function is strictly concave,
  so the second-order condition reduces to
  \(\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]/\partial e_{it}^2<0\). With a cost of
  evasion linear in \(e_{it}\) and \(\phi=0\), it becomes a restriction
  on the curvature of the detection probability alone (derivation in
  Appendix~\ref{sec-app-soc-model}):
  \begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-soc}{
  -\frac{q''(e_{it})}{q'(e_{it})}\,e_{it}<2
  }\end{equation}
\item
  The detection probability may be concave, but not too concave: its
  elasticity of \(q'\) with respect to \(e\) must stay above \(-2\). A
  linear \(q\) satisfies the condition everywhere (\(q''=0\)), as does
  any convex \(q\); a concave \(q\) such as \(1-e^{-\lambda e}\)
  satisfies it only for \(\lambda e<2\).
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Setting and Data}\label{sec-setting}

Colombian manufacturing in 1981--1991 offers a unique setting to study
tax evasion by overreporting deductible inputs. First, a sales tax that
worked as a value-added tax and rewarded overreporting materials:
manufacturers could reduce the tax they owed on their sales by claiming
fictitious deductions (Section~\ref{sec-setting-incentive}). Second, a
truth-reporting group of firms: corporations, which government and
market scrutiny kept from evading (Section~\ref{sec-setting-jo}). Third,
variation in the tax rate: the 1983 reform raised the sales-tax rate for
most industries but left food products exempt
(Section~\ref{sec-setting-st}). Finally, firm-level records of output
and inputs, together with legal form and taxes paid
(Section~\ref{sec-setting-data}). Because the approach uses the
production function, it applies to any data with firms' output and
inputs, such as surveys, administrative data, or tax records.

\section{Data}\label{sec-setting-data}

I use the Colombian Annual Survey of Manufacturing (EAM), a panel of
manufacturing plants with more than 10 employees from 1981 to
1991.\footnote{The survey's unit of observation is the plant; I refer to
  plants and firms interchangeably.} The EAM records, for each plant,
the output and inputs that a production function needs, the plant's
juridical organization, and the sales tax it charged on its sales and
paid on its purchases.\footnote{The year in the panel is the activity
  (reference) year. DANE runs the EAM each year with the economic year
  immediately preceding collection as the reference period (Departamento
  Administrativo Nacional de Estadística (DANE) 1995, 40): the survey
  for year \(x\) is collected during \(x+1\) and labelled \(x\)
  (e.g.~the 1992--1994 surveys were collected in 1993--1995, and the
  1992 file values assets at December 1992; Departamento Administrativo
  Nacional de Estadística (DANE) (2018)).} The panel is well known: it
has been used in seminal work on export dynamics, reallocation, and
productivity, and to estimate production functions (Roberts and Tybout
1997; Das et al. 2007; Eslava et al. 2004; Gandhi et al. 2020). Similar
to these studies, I observe inputs as firms reported them; in contrast,
I ask how the tax incentives of the period, including the 1983 reform,
shaped those reports. The working sample has 40,508 firm-years from
5,915 plants in 28 industries, covering the four juridical organizations
described in Section~\ref{sec-setting-jo} (Table~\ref{tbl-sum-stats}).

\section{The sales tax: a credit on
purchases}\label{sec-setting-incentive}

The Colombian sales tax worked as a value-added tax (VAT): manufacturers
credited the tax paid on their purchases against the tax charged on
their sales. The tax originally targeted the manufacturing sector, on
finished goods and imports. From 1966, manufacturers could claim credit
on the taxes paid on inputs \emph{incorporated} into the product, and
from 1974 on any purchase made by the firm, except the acquisition of
capital goods; the credits worked through a system of refunds (Perry and
Triana 1990, 180).

The credit rewards overreporting materials. An artificial increase in
the reported value of materials raises the credit by \(\tau_P\) pesos
per peso, the rate paid on purchases, and reduces the firm's tax
liability by as much. Capital goods carry no credit and labour is not a
taxed purchase, so I treat raw materials consumed, \(M^*_{it}\), as the
overreported input.

Enforcement was weak. Tax evasion in Colombia was high during the sample
period (Sánchez Torres and Gutiérrez Sourdis 1994), and the tax
authority had an inefficient auditing system, was overburdened, and
faced legal loopholes (Perry and Triana 1990).

\section{Juridical organizations: corporations as
non-evaders}\label{sec-setting-jo}

Corporations were the firms least likely to overreport, for three
reasons. First, the Superintendent of Corporations closely monitored
them by requiring an auditor. Second, freely tradable shares create an
incentive to be profitable: if a corporation falsely reports lower
profits, the value of its shares would likely fall, scaring away
shareholders and potential investors. Consistent with this, stock prices
of public corporations fall, on average, when news of their involvement
in tax shelters becomes public (Hanlon and Slemrod 2009; Slemrod 2019,
909, fn. 7). Third, shareholders pay the individual income tax only on
distributed dividends, so a corporation has an additional margin:
through its dividend policy, it decides when its owners pay.
Partnerships and LLCs have no such margin, since their owners are taxed
on all profits.

The data distinguish several juridical organizations; I keep four:
corporations, limited liability companies (LLCs), partnerships, and
proprietorships (Table~\ref{tbl-jo-summary}). Corporations
(\emph{sociedades anónimas}) are associations of capital, the
counterpart of the US corporation: at least five shareholders, tradable
shares of equal value, and liability limited to the capital contributed
(Joint Tax Program of the Organization of American States and the
Inter-American Development Bank 1965). An LLC (\emph{sociedad de
responsabilidad limitada}) has two or more partners, not exceeding 20
(Joint Tax Program of the Organization of American States and the
Inter-American Development Bank 1965) or 25 (Departamento Administrativo
Nacional de Estadística (DANE) 2018), whose shares cannot be traded and
whose liability is limited to the capital contributed; it is an
association of persons, not of capital, and is not subject to the
Superintendent of Corporations unless more than one-third of it is owned
by a corporation (Joint Tax Program of the Organization of American
States and the Inter-American Development Bank 1965). Partnerships are
associations of two or more persons who are jointly and severally liable
for the partnership's operations; they are a small group (3.5\% of
firm-years), pooled with the other unincorporated firms. Proprietorships
are individuals who allocate part of their assets to conduct commercial
activities.

come from taxes, fees, or special contributions.

The juridical organizations faced different income-tax rates. Since the
1974 reform, corporations were taxed at 40\% of their income, and
partnerships and LLCs at 20\% (Mclure 1989; Perry and Cárdenas 1986,
vol. 1, p.~23). Proprietors were subject to the graduated individual
schedule, with a top rate of 56\% (Perry and Cárdenas 1986, vol. 1,
p.~36). Owners were taxed again at the individual level, but
differently: shareholders paid tax only on the dividends they received,
whereas owners of partnerships and LLCs paid tax on their share of the
profits, whether distributed or not (Mclure 1989). The income of
proprietorships was taxed only once, at the individual level. Because
materials are a deductible cost, overreporting them also lowers the
income tax, so the reward to overreporting varied within industries too,
with the juridical organization.

\section{The 1983 reform}\label{sec-setting-st}

The 1983 reform raised the sales-tax rate for most of manufacturing but
left food products exempt. It combined the items previously taxed at 6
and 15 percent into a single 10 percent bracket (Perry and Triana 1990,
182), while food products (industries 311 and 312), exempt since the tax
was introduced (Perry and Triana 1990, 181), stayed exempt. For most
firms this was an increase: between 1983 and 1985, the median rate on
sales rose from 6\% to 10\%, and the median rate on purchases, the rate
at which each fictitious peso of materials is credited, from 6.6\% to
9.9\% (Table~\ref{tbl-st-by-year}).

The new rate applied from 1 April 1984 (Corte Constitucional de Colombia
2016; Perry and Triana 1990), so 1983 is the last full pre-reform year
and 1984 a partial one. The observed rates match this timing
(Table~\ref{tbl-st-by-year}). The median rate paid on sales is 6\% in
1981--1983, 10\% from 1985, and 12\% in 1991, after Ley 49 de 1990
raised the general rate to 12\% (Congreso de la República de Colombia
1990). In 1984 it is 9\%, exactly what three months at 6\% and nine
months at 10\% give (\(0.25\times6\%+0.75\times10\%=9\%\)).

The same law, Ley 9 de 1983, also cut income-tax rates. The individual
schedule, faced by proprietorships, fell by 4.6 percentage points on
average, with the top rate falling from 56\% to 49\%, and the LLC rate
fell from 20\% to 18\% (Perry and Cárdenas 1986, vol. 1, p.~36).

The reform followed a public debate. The Bird--Wiesner Mission
(1978--1981), a technical mission on Colombia's intergovernmental
finances led by Richard Bird and Eduardo Wiesner, reported in 1981
(Junguito and Rincón 2004); among its recommendations were better tax
administration to reduce evasion, stronger collection tools, wider
withholding, and more auditing (Bolaños Bolaños 2019). Using the
extraordinary powers granted by article 53 of Ley 9 de 1983, the
government issued Decreto 3541 on 29 December 1983. It set the general
sales-tax rate at 10\% (art. 6), extended the tax to the retail level as
a value-added tax, and took effect on 1 April 1984 (art. 92)
(Presidencia de la República de Colombia 1983; Perry and Triana 1990).

\chapter{Testing for Tax Evasion through
Overreporting}\label{sec-testing}

\section{Non-evaders}\label{sec-testing-nonevaders}

Because the firms' optimization decisions depend on the fiscal
environment, the identification strategy should be motivated by the
fiscal environment \(\Gamma\). In particular, the identification
strategy will be as good as how well we can tell apart a subset of firms
that have the highest incentive to not evade. For example, in the case
of Spain, the firms above the revenue LTU threshold. In the case of
Colombia, the corporations (Section~\ref{sec-setting-jo}).

\textbf{Assumption 1 (Non-evaders).} Based on the fiscal environment
\(\Gamma\), the researcher can identify a subset of firms
\(\theta_i\in\Theta^{NE}\subset \Theta\) that does not evade taxes by
overreporting inputs.

For those firms, then \(\mathbb{E}[u_{it}|\theta_i\in\Theta^{NE}]=0\).

In addition, I impose the following timing assumption.

\textbf{Assumption 2 (Independence).} The measurement error in output,
\(\varepsilon_{it}\), is independent of overreporting \(u_{it}\) and of
true materials \(M_{it}\), with \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{it}]=0\).

\section{Identifying the output elasticity of
materials}\label{sec-testing-beta}

Assume the production function is Cobb-Douglas,
\(M^{\beta}_{it}K_{it}^{\alpha_K}L_{it}^{\alpha_L}\exp(\omega_{it}+\varepsilon_{it})\).
The econometrician observes output, capital, labour, and prices of
intermediate inputs and output \(\{Y_{it},K_{it},L_{it},\rho_t, P_t\}\),
but she only observes overreported intermediate inputs,
\(M^*_{it}=M_{it}+e_{it}\). Let
\(u_{it}\equiv\ln(M^*_{it}/M_{it})=\ln(1+e_{it}/M_{it})\) denote the log
overreporting ratio.

From the materials first-order condition (Equation~\ref{eq-foc-m}), by
multiplying both sides by the intermediate inputs and dividing by the
output, we get

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-foc-cd}{
\begin{aligned}
    \ln\left(\frac{\rho_t M_{it}}{P_{t}Y_{it}}\right) &=\ln\beta - \varepsilon_{it} \\
    \ln\left(\frac{\rho_t M^*_{it}}{P_{t}Y_{it}}\right) &=\ln\beta - \varepsilon_{it}+u_{it}
\end{aligned}
}\end{equation}

where \(Y_{it}\) is output as measured, with measurement error
\(\varepsilon_{it}\). We can use Equation~\ref{eq-foc-cd} and
Assumptions 1 and 2 to recover the output elasticity of materials,
\(\beta\),

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-foc-cd-exp}{
    \ln\beta=\mathbb{E}\left[\ln\left(\frac{\rho_t M^*_{it}}{P_{t}Y_{it}}\right)\Bigg| \Theta^{NE}\right].
}\end{equation}

\section{The observed residual}\label{sec-testing-residual}

Having recovered the output elasticity of materials, \(\beta\), for all
firms, I can form the following variable using observed data.

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-ob-ev}{
    \mathcal V_{it}\equiv\ln\left(\frac{\rho_t M^*_{it}}{P_{t}Y_{it}}\right)-\ln\beta=u_{it}-\varepsilon_{it}
}\end{equation}

Tax evasion therefore can be recovered up to an independent random
variable.

\section{Testing for overreporting}\label{sec-testing-test}

Equation~\ref{eq-ob-ev} suggests a way to test the presence of tax
evasion through cost overreporting. Let
\(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}_{it}]\equiv \mu_{\mathcal{V}}\) and define the
null hypothesis as the absence of cost overreporting and the alternative
hypothesis as the presence of cost overreporting. Consequently, to
verify for the presence of tax evasion by overreporting, we can use a
one-sided test where under the alternative hypothesis
\(H_1: \mu_{\mathcal{V}}>0\), and under the null,
\(H_0: \mu_{\mathcal{V}}\le0\).

I invert the test. For each industry, Table~\ref{tbl-evasion-test}
displays the estimated mean of \(\mathcal V_{it}\) among unincorporated
firms and the values of \(\mu_{\mathcal V}\) in \([0,1]\) that the test
does not reject at the 5\% level, taking \(\ln\hat\beta\) from
corporations as the true value. The test detects overreporting where
this set excludes zero. Appendix~\ref{sec-app-test-robustness} reports a
conservative version of the test and a bootstrap test that carries the
sampling error of \(\ln\hat\beta\).

\chapter{Deconvolving Tax Evasion}\label{sec-deconvolution}

We can do better however. By Assumption 2, the measurement error
\(\varepsilon_{it}\) is independent of overreporting \(u_{it}\). Note
that, from Equation~\ref{eq-foc-cd}, we also recovered
\(f_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon)\), the distribution of \(\varepsilon\).
By using deconvolution methods, I can recover the tax evasion apart from
the output shock.

In particular, from probability theory, the density of the sum of two
\textbf{independent} random variables is equal to the
\textbf{convolution} of the densities of both addends (Meister 2009).
Hence, from Equation~\ref{eq-ob-ev},

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-conv}{
f_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathcal{V})=\int f_u(\mathcal{V}+\varepsilon)f_{\varepsilon}(\varepsilon)\text{d}\varepsilon
}\end{equation}

where \(f_{\mathcal{V}}\) denotes the density of \(\mathcal{V}\).

\section{Estimation}\label{sec-deconvolution-estimation}

Following Kang and Qiu (2021), consider the following logspline model:

\[
f_{u|\Theta}(u)=\exp(s(u;\theta)-C(\theta)), \quad L < u < R
\]

where \([L,R]\) define the support of \(f_u\),

\[
s(u;\theta)=\sum_{j=1}^{K-1}\theta_j B_j(u),
\]

\(\{B_j(u), j=1,2,\dots, K\}\) is a sequence of cubic B-spline basis
functions, and

\[
C(\theta) = \log\left(\int^R_L \exp(s(u;\theta)) \text{d}u \right) < \infty .
\]

Then \(f_{u|\Theta}(u)\) is a positive density function on \((L,R)\).
The logspline model assumes that \(f_u\) is a twice continuously
differentiable function. The last basis function is not used for
identifiability purposes, given that by the property of B-splines,
\(\sum_{j=1}^{K}B_j(u)=1\) for all \(u \in (L,R)\).

The log likelihood of the observed variable \(\mathcal{V}\) is

\[
\begin{aligned}
    l_{\mathcal{V}}(\mathbf{\theta})=&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log \left(\int f_{\varepsilon}(u-\mathcal{V_i})f_{u|\theta}(u)\text{d}u\right)\\
    =&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log \left(\int f_{\varepsilon}(u-\mathcal{V_i})\exp(s(u;\theta))\text{d}u\right)-nC(\theta)
\end{aligned}
\]

The usual maximum likelihood estimate \(\hat{\theta}\) is the maximizer
of \(l_{\mathcal{V}}(\theta)\).

To avoid overfitting, I add a penalty term to the log-likelihood
function. The penalty term is a linear combination of the squared second
derivatives of the B-spline basis functions. The second derivative of
the B-splines is approximated by finite differences (Eilers and Marx
1996). The penalty term is given by

\[
\text{Penalty}(\theta) = \lambda \cdot \|\mathbf{D}\theta \|^2
\]

Intuitively, we want to penalize excessive curvature in the log-density
\(\log f_u(u) \approx s(u; \theta) = \sum_j \theta_j B_j(u)\), where
\(B_j\) are B-spline basis functions.

\chapter{Production Function Parameters and Productivity}\label{sec-pf}

Here, I show how to recover the rest of the parameters of the production
function, including productivity and its Markov process. We can do it in
several ways, depending on our object of interest.

In the literature, it is not uncommon to assume that productivity
follows a Markov process. That is,

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-prod-markov}{
    \omega_{it}=g(\omega_{it-1})+\eta_{it}
}\end{equation}

Let \(\ln X_{it}=x_{it}\). With Equation~\ref{eq-ob-ev}, we can form the
following observed variable,

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-big-w}{
\begin{aligned}
    \mathcal W_{it} & \equiv y_{it} - \beta (m^*_{it}-\mathcal{V}_{it})\\
    & = y_{it} - \beta (m_{it}+u_{it}-u_{it}+\varepsilon_{it})\\
    & = y_{it} - \beta m_{it}-\beta\varepsilon_{it}\\
    & = \alpha_K k_{it}+\alpha_L l_{it}+\omega_{it}+(1-\beta)\varepsilon_{it}
\end{aligned}
}\end{equation}

Here, intuitively, we are trading unobservables, the unobserved tax
evasion for the measurement error. Also note that it is not necessary to
learn tax evasion to recover productivity. This is useful if a
practitioner is only interested in productivity and not in tax evasion.

By replacing \(\omega_{it}\) from Equation~\ref{eq-big-w} in
Equation~\ref{eq-prod-markov}, we get

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-big-w-reg}{
\begin{aligned}
    \mathcal W_{it}=(1-\beta)\varepsilon_{it}+\alpha_K k_{it}+\alpha_L l_{it}+g(\mathcal W_{it-1}-(1-\beta)\varepsilon_{it-1}-\alpha_K k_{it-1}-\alpha_L l_{it-1})+\eta_{it}
\end{aligned}
}\end{equation}

If \(g(\xi)=\gamma_0 +\gamma_1\xi\), to learn \(\alpha\) and \(g\), we
need to find instruments \(Z\) such that,

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-eta-moments}{
\mathbb{E}[\eta_{it}+(1-\beta)\varepsilon_{it}+\gamma_1(1-\beta)\varepsilon_{it-1}|Z]=0.
}\end{equation}

estimation noise in \(\omega_{it}\) biases towards zero. Part of the gap
in persistence, and so in half-lives, may reflect this difference rather
than overreporting.

\chapter{Fiscal Policy Evaluation: The 1983 Reform}\label{sec-fiscal}

Firms are known to overreport costs when tax enforcement tightens, which
raises the expected cost of evading, but less is known about the benefit
side: whether they overreport more when the tax rate, which sets how
much each peso of fictitious inputs saves, goes up. I investigate this
question and find evidence that input overreporting rises when the tax
rate increases. In 1983, Colombia raised the sales-tax rate in most
manufacturing industries while others remained exempt. Overreporting by
unincorporated firms rose after the reform, reaching about 8\% of true
materials by 1987, and the increase was concentrated in the industries
where the rate went up.

Whether firms evade more when tax rates rise matters for policymakers.
If evasion rises with the rate, a tax increase raises less revenue than
its mechanical effect suggests, and evasion affects the marginal
efficiency cost of raising tax rates (Slemrod 2019, 907). The concern is
especially relevant for sales and value-added taxes, whose credit on
purchases is a natural target of fictitious deductions (Carrillo et al.
2022). Yet recent empirical work says more about enforcement than about
tax rates.

The 1983 reform changed statutory rates rather than enforcement, which
makes it a rare setting in which to study this question. Governments
have agreed to randomize letters, audits and information reporting, but
not tax rates (Slemrod 2019), and among the studies Slemrod reviews, the
evidence on tax rates comes mostly from misreported imports (Fisman and
Wei 2004; Handley and Moore 2017). The same literature shows that firms
adjust reported costs: when tax authorities pushed firms to report more
revenue, Ecuadorian firms raised reported costs by 96 and Armenian firms
70--80 cents per dollar of additional revenue (Carrillo et al. 2017;
Asatryan and Peichl 2017).

In the model, the sales tax rate shifts the marginal benefit of
overreporting materials: a higher \(\tau\) raises the credit claimed on
each peso of fictitious inputs (Chapter~\ref{sec-model}). Although the
model has no income tax, the same logic extends to it: overreported
inputs also lower reported profits and, thus, the income-tax base. An
increase in either rate therefore raises the incentive to overreport
inputs, and a decrease lowers it.

The 1983 fiscal policy changed incentives \emph{across} industries and
\emph{within} industries. Across industries, the sales-tax (ST) rate
increased from 6\% to 10\% for most ST-liable industries, while
ST-exempt industries, food products, remained exempt
(Section~\ref{sec-setting-st}). Within industries, the income tax
changed by juridical organization (JO), and in the opposite direction.
\emph{Ley 9 de 1983} cut individual income-tax rates by 4.6 percentage
points on average across income brackets relative to the 1982 schedule,
and thus proprietorships faced lower rates (Perry and Cárdenas 1986).
LLCs received a smaller cut, from 20\% to 18\% (Perry and Cárdenas
1986). Overreporting inputs lowers both taxes, through a larger credit
on purchases and a smaller income-tax base, so the two changes pull the
incentive to overreport in opposite directions. In ST-liable industries,
the net incentive is therefore ambiguous for both: proprietorships were
compensated for much of the sales-tax increase through the income tax,
while LLCs were insufficiently compensated. In ST-exempt industries, the
income-tax cut alone lowered the incentive for proprietorships and
changed it little for LLCs, whose cut was small.

Because the income-tax cut differed between proprietorships and LLCs,
the reform's incentives also varied along the size distribution.
Proprietorships have a single owner and are the smallest. Tax
authorities find sole proprietorships and small businesses hard to
penetrate, and evasion in them is more sustainable (Slemrod 2019, 940).
At the median, LLCs have about two and a half times proprietorships'
labour and three times their gross output. LLCs have several owners and
non-tradable shares. In Spain, firms bunch just below the revenue
threshold above which the Large Taxpayers' Unit monitors them more
closely (Almunia and Lopez-Rodriguez 2018; Slemrod 2019, 925--26). At
the top, corporations are several times larger than LLCs in capital,
labour and gross output, and their shares are tradable. In addition, the
government keeps corporations under close supervision and requires them
to have an auditor. Lastly, since evidence suggests stock prices of
public companies fall when news of their involvement in tax shelters
becomes public (Hanlon and Slemrod 2009; Slemrod 2019, 909),
corporations might have lower incentives to evade. In this chapter
corporations serve as the benchmark of non-evaders, on the assumption
that audited statements, third-party reporting and the market's reaction
keep them from overreporting.

\section{Empirical approach}\label{sec-fiscal-approach}

From the materials FOC, for corporations, the truth-reporters, \(i\) in
industry \(j\) at year \(t\), the log share of materials' cost over
sales is

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-fiscal-corp}{
s_{ijt}^{C} = \ln\beta_j-\varepsilon_{ijt}
}\end{equation}

where
\(s_{ijt}\equiv\ln\big((1-\tau_{P,ijt})\rho_tM^*_{ijt}/((1-\tau_{S,ijt})P_tY_{ijt})\big)\)
is the log share of materials' cost over sales, both net of sales taxes
(Appendix~\ref{sec-app-fiscal-wedge}), \(\beta_j\) is the output
elasticity of materials of industry \(j\), and \(\varepsilon_{ijt}\) is
the error term. For unincorporated firms, the evaders,

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-fiscal-unincorp}{
s_{ijt}^{N} = \ln\beta_j + u_{ijt} - \varepsilon_{ijt}
}\end{equation}

where \(u_{ijt}=\ln(1+e_{ijt}/M_{ijt})\) is the overreporting of
materials' cost due to tax evasion.

Let \(D_{ijt}^N\) be a dummy indicating whether the firm is an
unincorporated firm. Then,

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-fiscal-stack}{
\begin{aligned}
  (1-D_{ijt}^N)s_{ijt} + D_{ijt}^N s_{ijt} =& (1-D_{ijt}^N)(\ln\beta_j-\varepsilon_{ijt}) + D_{ijt}^N \big(\ln\beta_j+u_{ijt}  - \varepsilon_{ijt}\big) \\
    s_{ijt} =& \ln\beta_j + D_{ijt}^N  u_{ijt} - \varepsilon_{ijt}
\end{aligned}
}\end{equation}

Taking expectations,

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-fiscal-mean}{
E[s_{ijt}|D^{N}=0,j]=\ln\beta_j, \qquad E[s_{ijt}|D^{N}=1,j]-E[s_{ijt}|D^{N}=0,j]= \mu
}\end{equation}

identifies the average overreporting level,
\(\mu\equiv E[u_{ijt}\mid D^N_{ijt}=1]\), a population expectation; its
estimate is \(\hat\mu\).

In the case of the fiscal policy changes, I am interested in the changes
in average tax evasion after the 1983 fiscal reform. Taking the
difference of the expectations between year \(t'\) and \(t=1983\),

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-fiscal-diff}{
\begin{aligned}
    s_{ijt'} - s_{ij,1983}
     &= D_{ijt}^N  (u_{ijt'} - u_{ij,1983}) - (\varepsilon_{ijt'} - \varepsilon_{ij,1983}) \\
    E[s_{ijt'}-s_{ij,1983}|D^{N}=1] &= \mu_{t'} - \mu_{1983} \equiv \Delta\mu_{t'}
\end{aligned}
}\end{equation}

where \(\mu_t\) is the annual average tax evasion of year \(t\), and
\(\Delta\mu_{t}\) is the change in average tax evasion between year
\(t\) and 1983, the last full year before the reform took effect
(Section~\ref{sec-setting-st}).

The identifying assumptions are explicit in
Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-stack} and Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-diff}: (i)
corporations do not overreport (Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-corp}); (ii)
firms within an industry share a common Cobb-Douglas technology, with a
constant output elasticity of materials \(\beta_j\)
(Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-corp} and Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-unincorp});
and (iii) the ex-post shock \(\varepsilon_{ijt}\) is independent of
evasion, true materials and juridical organization, with
\(E[\varepsilon_{ijt}\mid D^N_{ijt},t]=0\).

Even though Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-diff} resembles a traditional
difference-in-differences design, the result does not require parallel
trends for identification. Under (i)-(iii),
Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-stack} and Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-diff} show
that the difference between unincorporated firms and corporations in
year \(t\) is the average overreporting itself, \(\mu_t\), and changes
in \(\mu_t\) over time, including before 1983, are changes in evasion.

To estimate Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-stack}, I use the following
regression specification,

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-fiscal-lvl-reg}{
    s_{ijt} = \ln\beta_j + \sum_{t=1981}^{1991} \mu_t\, D_{ijt}^N\,\mathbb 1[\text{year}=t] + \zeta_{ijt}
}\end{equation}

where \(\mu_{t}\) is the average tax evasion of unincorporated firms in
year \(t\); \(\ln\beta_j\) enter as industry fixed effects, estimated
jointly with the \(\mu_t\), so no first-stage estimate enters the
regression and its standard errors need no bootstrap or
generated-regressor correction; and
\(\zeta_{ijt}=\nu_{ijt}-\varepsilon_{ijt}\), where
\(u_{ijt} = \mu_t + \nu_{ijt}\) and \(\nu_{ijt}\) is the idiosyncratic
component of tax evasion that is not captured by the year dummies.
Likewise, I estimate Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-diff} with the following
regression,

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-fiscal-diff-reg}{
    s_{ijt} = \ln\beta_j + \mu\, D_{ijt}^N+\sum_{t=1981}^{1991} \mathbb{1}[t\neq1983]\,\Delta\mu_t\, D_{ijt}^N\,\mathbb 1[\text{year}=t] + \zeta_{ijt}
}\end{equation}

where \(\Delta\mu_{t}\) is the difference in tax evasion of
unincorporated firms between year \(t\) and 1983, the last full year
before the reform took effect, and \(\mu\) is the average tax evasion of
unincorporated firms in 1983. The outcome is the share net of sales
taxes, so the estimates exclude the mechanical effect of the rate change
on the share; Appendix~\ref{sec-app-fiscal-wedge} estimates that effect.

The 1983 fiscal reform changed incentives to evade taxes over time
\emph{differently} for firms in ST-liable and ST-exempt industries. Most
ST-liable industries faced an ST rate \emph{increase} from 6\% to 10\%,
while ST-exempt industries \emph{remained exempt} before and after the
reform. Note that firms in \emph{all} industries are subject to the
income tax, which also generates incentives to evade taxes by
overreporting input costs: lower reported profits due to higher input
costs reduce the tax base, and thus the tax liability. Therefore, the
income tax and the ST generate tax evasion incentives for firms in
ST-liable industries, while only the income tax does for firms in
ST-exempt industries. For unincorporated firms,

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-fiscal-noncorp}{
\begin{aligned}
s_{ijt}^{N} &= \ln\beta_j + u^{ST}_{ijt} + u^{IT}_{ijt}-\varepsilon_{ijt} & j &\in \text{ST-Liable} \\
s_{ijt}^{N} &= \ln\beta_j + u^{IT}_{ijt}-\varepsilon_{ijt} & j &\in \text{ST-Exempt}
\end{aligned}
}\end{equation}

where \(u^{ST}_{ijt}\) and \(u^{IT}_{ijt}\) stand for the overreporting
due to the ST and the income tax (IT), respectively. Let \(D^E_{ijt}\)
be a dummy indicating whether the industry is ST-exempt and
\(D^{L}_{ijt}\) a dummy indicating whether the industry is ST-liable,
such that \(D^E_{ijt} + D^{L}_{ijt} = 1\). Combining
Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-corp} with Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-noncorp},

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-fiscal-lvl-split}{
    s_{ijt} = \ln\beta_j + D_{ijt}^N D^E_{ijt}\, u^{IT}_{ijt} + D_{ijt}^N D^{L}_{ijt} (u^{ST}_{ijt} + u^{IT}_{ijt}) - \varepsilon_{ijt}
}\end{equation}

Taking expectations,

\[
\begin{aligned}
E[s_{ijt}|D^{N}=1,D^E=1,j]-\ln\beta_j &= \mu^{IT}_E \\
E[s_{ijt}|D^{N}=1,D^{L}=1,j]-\ln\beta_j &= \mu^{ST}_{L}+\mu^{IT}_{L}\equiv\mu^{ST+IT}_{L}
\end{aligned}
\]

identifies the average overreporting by firms in ST-exempt industries,
\(\mu^{IT}_E\), and in ST-liable industries, \(\mu^{ST+IT}_{L}\). If I
assume \(\mu^{IT}_E = \mu^{IT}_{L}\), I can also identify the
overreporting due to ST, \(\mu^{ST}_{L}\). Since income-tax rates depend
on the juridical organization and not on the industry, I assume that the
income tax generates the same incentives to evade taxes across
industries, whether they are exempt of ST or not. Because I aggregate
different juridical organizations in exempt and liable industries into
unincorporated firms, this assumption might break if the composition of
juridical organizations is non-trivially different between exempt and
liable industries. The regression versions of
Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-lvl-reg} and Equation~\ref{eq-fiscal-diff-reg}
interact the year dummies with \(D^N_{ijt}D^E_{ijt}\) and
\(D^N_{ijt}D^L_{ijt}\), giving one path per industry type, \(\mu_{E,t}\)
and \(\mu_{L,t}\) (and \(\Delta\mu_{E,t}\), \(\Delta\mu_{L,t}\) relative
to 1983). Splitting unincorporated firms further by JO, into LLCs and
proprietorships, gives four such paths.

\subsection{Changes in true materials cannot explain the
pattern}\label{sec-fiscal-m}

Since \(u_{ijt}=\ln(1+e_{ijt}/M_{ijt})\), the empirical exercise remains
valid as long as changes in \(s\) are not explained by changes in \(M\)
(or \(\varepsilon\)), but only by \(e\) or \(e/M\): changes in the proxy
should be explained by changes to the incentives to adjust
overreporting, not by firms adjusting their optimal choice of true
inputs.

The alternative argument is that changes in true inputs explain the
increase in the overreporting share. Suppose \(e\) is unchanged. Then,
after the reform, unincorporated firms would have to lower their true
inputs, by either an increase in relative prices or productivity shocks.
Productivity shocks are unrelated to tax rates, so this is unlikely.
According to the FOC, taxes do not show in the optimal choice of firms.
Besides, the increase after the reform is gradual, while the reform made
only a one-time change. So this alternative argument does not hold. The
evasion channel is more direct: an increase in the sales tax rate leads
to an increase in the overreporting share because a higher tax rate
increases the marginal benefit of overreporting.

Overreporting rose once the reform raised the benefit of overreporting
in most industries: after the sales-tax increase took effect, input
overreporting by unincorporated firms increased gradually
(Figure~\ref{fig-fiscal-all}, Table~\ref{tbl-fiscal-all}). Input
overreporting reached an average of about 9\% of true materials in 1987,
where it stabilized until 1990.

The standard errors of the levels and of the differences over time look
very different. In levels, the large standard errors suggest that tax
evasion is widely heterogeneous across firms. This heterogeneity may
come from differences in the tax rate due to firm characteristics
(industry, JO, and exports), and in the cost of evasion across firms.
The differences over time, however, are tight for a mechanical reason.
Every year's level \(\hat\mu_t\) is estimated against the same baseline,
so estimation error in that baseline enters every year with the same
sign, and the level estimates are strongly positively correlated. Since
\(\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu_t-\hat\mu_{1983})=\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu_t)+\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu_{1983})-2\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\mu_t,\hat\mu_{1983})\),
this shared error cancels in the differences. The correlation affects
inference only, not the consistency of the estimates: the regressors are
group-by-year dummies, and
\(E[\zeta_{ijt}\mid D^N_{ijt},\text{year}]=0\) because \(\varepsilon\)
is independent of \((e,M)\) and \(\nu_{ijt}\) is, by construction, the
deviation from the group-year mean. Standard errors are two-way
clustered by plant and year.

\subsection{Overreporting before the reform}\label{sec-fiscal-pre}

Corporations are a clean comparison group that will not react to the
policy changes, because the assumption is that these firms do not evade
taxes by overreporting. Thus, I can interpret the estimates as the
change in overreporting relative to 1983, the last full year before the
reform took effect.

The policy changes of the time were the result of a public debate
following the 1981 report of the Bird--Wiesner Mission, a technical
mission on Colombia's intergovernmental finances led by Richard Bird
(international tax expert) and Eduardo Wiesner (Colombian economist)
that recommended better tax administration to reduce evasion (Junguito
and Rincón 2004; Bolaños Bolaños 2019). The reform was enacted at the
end of December 1983 and took effect on 1 April 1984
(Section~\ref{sec-setting-st}). These changes aimed to reduce the fiscal
deficit without resorting to money financing and to reactivate
investment through tax incentives, while broadening the tax base and
simplifying collection (Sánchez Torres and Gutiérrez Sourdis 1994).

A plausible scenario is that firms could have reduced their
overreporting during the public debate as a way to signal compliance and
avoid further tax increases, and then reverted their behaviour after the
reform was enacted. If this is the case, then part of the increase in
overreporting after 1983 is a return to previous levels of tax evasion.
More data points before 1981 would help to clarify this issue.

exempt industries mainly faced a decrease in their entity-level income
tax. This decrease would lead to lower incentives to evade taxes.
However, since the reduction is small, it is not unreasonable to find
only small changes in tax evasion for these industries. In ST-liable
industries, LLCs faced two opposing effects: the increase in sales tax
rates, which would increase incentives to evade taxes, and the
income-tax cut, which would decrease incentives to evade taxes. The
evidence suggests that the increase in sales tax rates dominates the
effect of the income-tax cut, leading to a significant increase in tax
evasion for LLCs in ST-liable industries.

Proprietorships show a more heterogeneous response
(Figure~\ref{fig-fiscal-prt}, Table~\ref{tbl-fiscal-prt}). In ST-liable
industries, their overreporting barely changed in 1984--1986, consistent
with the income-tax cut compensating them for much of the sales-tax
increase; it rose from 1987, peaking in 1988. In ST-exempt industries,
their overreporting rose after 1983, even though they faced only the
income-tax cut. This result is unexpected, and I offer two possible
explanations. First, the incentive to overreport depends on the marginal
tax rate, not the average one, and the 1983 cut in marginal rates was
much smaller than the cut in average rates at both ends of the income
distribution: close to zero at low incomes and about 2 percentage points
at high incomes (Table~\ref{tbl-marg-tax-1983}). Second, the 1982--1983
changes stopped individuals from fully deducting their financial
expenses (Ocampo and Perry 1983), which gave proprietorships, but not
LLCs, a reason to replace a lost deduction with overreported inputs.

Proprietorships' standard errors are also several times those of LLCs,
which suggests that they responded less uniformly. This is plausible:
proprietorships range from family workshops to young entrepreneurial
firms, with varying degrees of process standardization, record-keeping,
while LLCs tend to be larger firms with more established processes, and
their responses look more homogeneous.

\section{Takeaways and link to the structural
model}\label{sec-fiscal-takeaways}

Input overreporting rises with the marginal benefit of overreporting.
After the 1983 reform raised the sales tax, overreporting by
unincorporated firms increased, reaching about 9\% of true materials by
1987, and the increase was concentrated in ST-liable industries, where
the benefit rose. Within those industries it rose most among LLCs, whose
small income-tax cut left most of the sales-tax increase in place, while
proprietorships, compensated by a larger income-tax cut, barely
responded at first. The one result that does not fit, the rise among
proprietorships in ST-exempt industries, has two possible explanations
in the reform's other provisions (Section~\ref{sec-fiscal-jo}).

This evidence supports the model's key comparative static,
\(\partial e^*/\partial\tau>0\) (Chapter~\ref{sec-model}), which the
structural estimation cannot test by regression: overreporting and the
tax rate are both built from the same reported materials, so a
regression of one on the other is mechanically biased. It also supports
the identification of the detection probability in
Chapter~\ref{sec-counterfactual}, which rests on the same source of
variation: changes in the sales-tax benefit of overreporting move
evasion.

\chapter{The Counterfactual: Claimed Deductions and the Tax
Rate}\label{sec-counterfactual}

\section{Partial Equilibrium Claimed-Deductions
Curve}\label{sec-cf-question}

How do tax revenues change as the government changes the sales tax rate?
As the tax rate increases, the incentives to claim additional fictitious
deductions increase, but an increase in evasion raises the probability
of detection. Therefore, firms face a trade-off. On the flip side, a
decrease in the tax rate reduces the incentives to evade and firms start
to decrease the level of overreporting (intensive margin) and some firms
start jumping out of evasion altogether (extensive margin). For the
government, taking the current observed state as given, what is the
marginal change in revenue as the tax rate changes?

I study this question using the estimates of the structural objects from
the firms' tax evasion model. With the parameters at hand, I construct a
partial-equilibrium claimed-deductions curve: expected claimed
deductions as a function of the tax rate. Claimed deductions are the
channel through which evasion moves revenue; every extra peso claimed is
a peso of revenue lost.

The curve rises gently on the tax-cut side and steeply on the
tax-increase side. A 0.5\% increase in the rate already raises claimed
deductions significantly, by 16--28\%, lowering revenue, while claimed
deductions fall significantly below their current level only after a cut
of at least 8\%.

Given the firm's optimization problem, the government's expected tax
revenue is,

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-govt-rev}{
R_i(\Delta;M_i) = \tilde\tau_{it}\big(P_tY_{it} - \big[M_i+(1-q(e'_i(\Delta)))e'_i(\Delta)\big]\big)
}\end{equation}

where \(\tilde\tau_{it}\equiv(1+\Delta)\tau_{it}\). The first term in
Equation~\ref{eq-govt-rev} is the revenue collected from sales taxes on
sales, \(\tilde\tau_{it}P_tY_{it}\). The second term is the credits
claimed on sales taxes on purchases. The term includes the firms' true
inputs, \(M_i\), plus the counterfactual undetected evasion,
\((1-q(e'_i(\Delta)))e'_i(\Delta)\), at tax rate percentage change
\(\Delta\). The firm's FOC shows \(\tau\) directly modifies the marginal
benefit of evasion leading to changes in the optimal evasion
\(e'_i(\Delta)\).

In general, governments can also choose the level of enforcement effort,
and therefore they can affect \(q(\cdot)\). In addition, governments
might collect additional income by penalizing firms caught evading.

Since I lack information on these margins, I hold output and input
decisions fixed. The tax on sales then changes only mechanically with
the rate, and the behavioural response of revenue runs entirely through
claimed deductions, so I focus on

\[
-\tilde\tau_{it}\big[M_i+(1-q(e'_i(\Delta)))e'_i(\Delta)\big]
\]

\section{Estimating the model}\label{estimating-the-model}

\subsection{Setup}\label{setup-1}

Using estimates of the production function parameters
\((\beta,\alpha_K, \alpha_L)\) and constructed residuals,
\(\mathcal V_{it}\), \(\tilde{\mathcal W}_{it}\) from
Chapter~\ref{sec-deconvolution} and Chapter~\ref{sec-pf}, the objective
is to recover the parameters of the probability of detection and the
cost of evasion function \((q(e),\kappa(e,\omega))\).

As described in Chapter~\ref{sec-model}, I follow the classical tax
evasion literature building on the seminal work of Allingham and Sandmo
(1972). In this literature, the probability of detection, \(q(e)\), is
increasing in the evasion level. This assumption is consistent with a
tax authority that audits a firm whenever its overreporting exceeds a
random threshold.\footnote{The probability of detection might also
  depend on enforcement effort \(a\), \(q(e,a)\). However, since I do
  not observe enforcement, I treat it as constant across firms and time.}
The cost of evasion, \(\kappa_{it}(e,\omega)\), represents the resources
of the firm allocated to the evasion endeavour that could have been
employed in a productive activity for the firm. The cost of evasion is
increasing in the evasion level and convex in productivity. The \(\psi\)
term, an exogenous cost shock, allows cross-firm heterogeneity.

The cost of evasion, convex in productivity, rationalizes the observed
pattern in Carrillo et al. (2022), where evasion is increasing along the
size distribution of firms but drops drastically at the very top. In the
production function literature, firms are typically assumed to share a
common technology and differ only in their productivity, and thus
productivity represents a major component of size. In the model, two
primitives govern firms' evasion decisions: the probability of detection
and the cost of evasion. The probability of detection cannot explain the
increase in evasion along the size distribution. Holding the cost of
evasion constant, the probability of detection would have to decrease as
firms grow in size, meaning bigger firms are less likely to be audited,
which does not make economic sense. If anything, the probability of
detection could remain flat along the size distribution for all but the
very largest firms; higher scrutiny of the very largest firms could add
to the drop at the top, but it cannot produce the rise below it.

In contrast, the pattern can be rationalized by the cost of evasion
being an economic opportunity cost. Take, for example, the manager's
time. For small firms, managers commonly multitask across different
productive activities, such as sales, HR, purchasing, and administrative
tasks. For these firms, allocating time to tax evasion is costly. As
firms increase in size and resources, managers delegate activities
according to the firm's division of labour. For large firms, allocating
some of the manager's time to evasion is not as costly. However, as
firms grow past a certain threshold, departments grow in resources,
including the number of employees. Allocating resources to any single
activity requires coordination among a larger number of employees in
different departments. For example, the records from the warehouse,
accounting, purchasing, and finance must match for every fake invoice
used to claim deductions. Therefore, the cost of evasion might decrease
as firms get bigger but increase past a certain threshold.

Linear detection-probability and evasion-cost functions guarantee a
unique interior solution for the evasion level (see
Appendix~\ref{sec-app-soc-model}). In particular, I assume these
functions to be,

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-prob-detect}{
q(e)=\min\{\lambda e,1\}
}\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-eva-cost}{
\kappa_{it}(e_{it}, \omega_{it})=e_{it}\textbf{e}^{\delta_0-\delta_1\omega_{it}+\delta_2\omega^2_{it}+\psi_{it}}
}\end{equation}

Using the functional forms defined above, the first-order condition with
respect to tax evasion for evaders (interior solution) becomes,

\[
\tau\rho_t(1-2\lambda e_{it}) = \textbf{e}^{\delta_0-\delta_1\omega_{it}+\delta_2\omega^2_{it}+\psi_{it}}
\]

Let \[
h(e_{it};\lambda)\;\equiv\;\ln(\tau\rho_t)+\ln(1-2\lambda e_{it}),
\qquad e_{it}\in(0,\frac{1}{2\lambda}),
\]

Taking logs, the evasion FOC becomes,

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-foc-psi}{
\psi_{it}\;=\;h(e_{it};\lambda)-\delta_0+\delta_1\omega_{it}-\delta_2\omega^2_{it}
}\end{equation}

The parameters of interest are
\(\theta=(\lambda,\delta_0,\delta_1,\delta_2)\).

\subsection{Estimation}\label{sec-counter-estimation}

Using the firm-level constructed variables from
Chapter~\ref{sec-deconvolution} and Chapter~\ref{sec-pf}, I cast the
estimation problem in terms of a single unobservable, true materials
\(M_{it}\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
e(M)=M^*_{it}-M,\qquad 
\varepsilon(M)=\ln(M^*_{it}/M)-\mathcal V_{it},\qquad
\omega(M)=\tilde{\mathcal W}_{it}-(1-\beta)\varepsilon(M)
\end{aligned}
\]

Therefore, I rewrite Equation~\ref{eq-foc-psi} as,

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-psi-of-m}{
\psi(M)\;=\;h\big(e(M)\big)-\delta_0+\delta_1\omega(M)-\delta_2\omega(M)^2
}\end{equation}

In addition to depending on a single unobservable, \(M_{it}\), the setup
presents two non-standard features. First, it is nonlinear and
nonseparable in \(M\) through the log term in \(h\), and thus a
traditional method of moments cannot help (one cannot interact with an
instrument to make the undesired error term go away). Second, the domain
of \(M\) depends on the parameter being estimated. The support of the
latent variable depends on \(\lambda\) because the FOC establishes the
ceiling \(e<\tfrac 1{2\lambda}\) and restricts true materials to
\(M\in(M^*_{it}-\tfrac 1{2\lambda}, M^*_{it})\).

The method \emph{Entropic Latent Variable Integration via Simulation}
(ELVIS) (Schennach 2014) is built for exactly this combination of
features.

Let \(f_Z\) denote the density of the observed data \(Z_{it}\), and let
\(f_{M|Z}\) denote a candidate conditional density of \(M_{it}\) given
\(Z_{it}\), so that \(f_{M|Z}f_Z\) is the implied joint density of
\((M_{it},Z_{it})\).\footnote{Schennach (2014) states the result for
  general probability measures. I write it with densities because, in
  this application, \(M_{it}\) is continuous on the known support
  \(\big(\max\{0,M^*_{it}-\tfrac{1}{2\lambda}\},M^*_{it}\big)\); every
  candidate conditional distribution therefore has a density there. The
  reference density \(f^0_{M|Z}\) used in estimation is the uniform
  density on that support: the estimator draws \(M_{it}\) uniformly on
  the support and accepts each draw with probability
  \(\min\{1,\exp[\gamma'(g_{\text{new}}-g_{\text{old}})]\}\); the
  chain's average therefore computes \(\tilde g\) in
  Equation~\ref{eq-elvis-tilt}.} The question is whether some choice of
\(f_{M|Z}\) makes \(E_{f_{M|Z}f_Z}[g(M,Z,\theta)]=0\).

Schennach (2014) shows the abstract \emph{existence} problem of
\(f_{M|Z}\) is equivalent to a more concrete optimization problem (her
Theorem 2.1):

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-elvis-equiv}{
\inf_{f_{M|Z}}\big\|E_{f_{M|Z}f_Z}[g(M,Z,\theta)]\big\|=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \inf_{\gamma\in\mathbb R^{d_g}}\big\|E_{f_Z}[\tilde g(Z,\theta,\gamma)]\big\|=0
}\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-elvis-tilt}{
\tilde g(Z,\theta,\gamma)\equiv\frac{\int g(M,Z,\theta)\exp\big(\gamma'g(M,Z,\theta)\big)\,f^0_{M|Z}(M|Z)\,dM}{\int \exp\big(\gamma'g(M,Z,\theta)\big)\,f^0_{M|Z}(M|Z)\,dM}
}\end{equation}

Among the conditional distributions of \(M\) given \(Z\) that satisfy
the moment conditions, ELVIS selects the one with maximum entropy
relative to an arbitrary reference density \(f^0_{M|Z}\). In the
optimization problem, \(\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{d_g}\) is the Lagrange
multiplier vector on the moment constraints. \(f^0_{M|Z}\) only needs to
be positive wherever the true conditional density of \(M\) is; any
choice with the right support gives the same \(\hat\theta\) (Remark 2.3,
Schennach 2014). In this setting, this allows me to avoid specifying
\(f_\psi\) or \(f_\varepsilon\) or searching over the space of all
conditional distributions of \(M\). Instead, I solve a finite
optimization problem.

The true (unobserved) conditional distribution of \(M\) satisfies the
moment condition at the true \(\theta_0\), so Theorem 2.1 guarantees
some \(\gamma\) also solves the right-hand side of
Equation~\ref{eq-elvis-equiv} at \(\theta_0\). Searching over \(\theta\)
for the value where this finite-dimensional problem has a solution
therefore recovers \(\theta_0\), regardless of the chosen \(f^0_{M|Z}\),
without requiring knowledge of the true conditional distribution of
\(M\).

The entropy-maximizing distribution satisfies \(E[g(M,Z,\theta_0)]=0\),
but it should not be treated as the actual distribution of \(M\), since
many conditional distributions of \(M\) given \(Z\) satisfy the moment
condition (Schennach 2022, 1250). This matters for the counterfactual
question. Since expected counterfactual revenue depends on the latent
\(M_{it}\), it would be tempting to average it over draws from such a
distribution. Instead, I include the counterfactual object as an
auxiliary parameter with its own moment condition and estimate it
jointly with \(\theta\) and \(\gamma\).

During estimation, I use the model's independence assumptions:
\(\varepsilon\) is independent of \((M,e,\omega)\) and \(\psi\) is
independent of \((\omega,M)\), with \(E[\varepsilon]=E[\psi]=0\). They
imply the following set of moments,

\[
g(M,Z,\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}
\psi(M,Z) \\
\varepsilon(M,Z) \\
\psi(M,Z)\ln M \\
\psi(M,Z)\omega(M,Z) \\
\psi(M,Z)\omega(M,Z)^2 \\
\varepsilon(M,Z)\ln M \\
\varepsilon(M,Z) e(M,Z) \\
\varepsilon(M,Z)\omega(M,Z) \\
\frac{\partial h(e,\lambda)}{\partial\lambda}\,\varepsilon(M,Z)
\end{bmatrix}
\]

The nine moments are combined into a single test statistic through the
continuously updated estimator (CUE) objective \(\hat L_n(\theta)\) of
Schennach (2014)'s Definition F.1. Her Theorem F.1 shows that, at any
\(\theta\) satisfying the moment condition,
\(TS(\theta)=2n\hat L_n(\theta)\) is asymptotically bounded above by a
\(\chi^2_{d_g}\) random variable. Comparing \(TS(\theta)\) with the
\(\chi^2_{d_g}\) critical value therefore gives a conservative test,
whose size is at most its nominal level; I call it the conservative test
throughout and report as confidence sets the tested points it does not
reject. For the nine moments, the 95\% critical value is
\(\chi^2_{9,.95}=16.92\). The exact objective and my sign convention
relative to hers are in Appendix~\ref{sec-app-elvis}.

The estimation sample has 32,232 unincorporated firm-periods (3,340 at
the \(\tau=0\) corner), after dropping the 0.5\% of non-corner
firm-periods with the largest reported materials
(Appendix~\ref{sec-app-elvis}).

\subsection{Discussion}\label{discussion}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{\(\hat\lambda\) is very small}: a few aggressive evaders
  dominate the estimate; the model is trying to fit a straight line over
  a highly right-skewed distribution.

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Mechanically, selecting a functional form for the probability of
    detection imposes a ceiling probability
  \item
    Assuming all unincorporated firms with a non-zero sales tax rate on
    purchases evade taxes implies that all firms evade below the ceiling
    (normalization)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  \textbf{(\(\hat\delta_1, \hat\delta_2\))}: seem to be consistently
  identified by the curvature of the evasion distribution and
  productivity \(\omega\). \(\hat\omega^*\) is stable across the grid
  searches in Appendix~\ref{sec-app-elvis}, and
  \((\hat\delta_1,\hat\delta_2)\) have the expected signs at every
  tested point
\end{itemize}

\section{Counterfactual design}\label{sec-cf-design}

Let the counterfactual tax rate be
\(\tilde\tau_{it}=(1+\Delta)\tau_{it}\). Then, given the parameter
estimates, the counterfactual evasion is,

\[
e'_i(\Delta)=\max\left(0,\ \frac{\tilde\tau_{it}\rho_t-C(\omega_i,\psi_i)}{2\lambda\,\tilde\tau_{it}\rho_t}\right),\qquad C(\omega,\psi)\equiv\textbf{e}^{\delta_0-\delta_1\omega+\delta_2\omega^2+\psi}
\]

Firms might jump out of evasion and optimally select \(e'_i=0\) as
\(\Delta\) adjusts the marginal benefit of evasion. This decision
justifies \(\max(0,\cdot)\).

Substituting the baseline FOC, \(e'_i\) can be written in terms of the
firm's baseline evasion \(e_i\),

\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-counter-e}{
e'_i(\Delta)=\max\!\big(0,\frac{1}{1+\Delta}[e_i+\Delta/(2\lambda)]\big)
}\end{equation}

where \(e_i=M^*_i-M_i\) is the firm's baseline evasion at the current
state, a function of the latent \(M_i\), and captures the firm
heterogeneity defined by \((\omega, \psi)\). Equation~\ref{eq-counter-e}
shows that the counterfactual evasion is linear in the model's current
evasion with slope \(1/(1+\Delta)\) and an intercept,
\(\Delta/[2\lambda(1+\Delta)]\), constant across firms.

The counterfactual evasion is a function of the same latent \(M_i\) as
the main moments system. As briefly mentioned in
Section~\ref{sec-counter-estimation}, define firm \(i\)'s counterfactual
deduction claims as
\(\text{Claims}_i(\Delta)\equiv\tilde\tau_{it}\big[M_i+(1-q(e'_i(\Delta)))e'_i(\Delta)\big]\),
the credit claimed on true inputs plus undetected overreporting. I
include an auxiliary parameter \(\theta_C(\Delta)\), mean deduction
claims, with its own moment
\(E\big[\text{Claims}_i(\Delta)-\theta_C(\Delta)\big]=0\). Claims are
measured in nominal COP; each firm-period's claims are deflated by the
GDP deflator of its year before averaging, so \(\theta_C(\Delta)\) is
real mean claims per firm-period. Therefore, the number of moments
increases to \(d_g=10\).

To obtain the confidence set of \(\theta_C(\Delta)\), I invert the test.
At each \(\Delta\), \(\theta\) is fixed at the best point from
Section~\ref{sec-cf-estimates} and only \(\gamma\) is re-optimized
(Aguiar and Kashaev 2020). I grid over candidate \(\theta_C\) and build
the confidence set
\(\{\theta_C: TS(\Delta,\theta_C)<\chi^2_{10,.95}=18.31\}\).

\item
  Mechanism for the asymmetry:
  \(e'_i(\Delta)=[e_i+\Delta/(2\lambda)]/(1+\Delta)\) is a horse race
  between \(e_i\) and the constant term \(\Delta/(2\lambda)\). With
  \(\hat\lambda\) tiny, the constant wins: an increase shifts every firm
  by about the same large amount; a cut removes evasion firm by firm
  (small evaders hit zero first), so the aggregate response is gradual.
\item
  This is a property of linear \(q\) and linear \(\kappa\) combined with
  small \(\hat\lambda\), not of linearity alone. Convex
  \(q=(\lambda e)^k\) and concave \(q=1-e^{-\lambda e}\) break the
  uniform-shift result .
\end{itemize}

\section{Robustness}\label{sec-cf-robust}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Rejected alternative target: only the undetected-overreporting part of
  claimed deductions, \(\tilde\tau_{it}(1-q(e'_i))e'_i\), leaving out
  the credit on true inputs. For cuts beyond about 2\%, most firms stop
  evading (\(e'_i=0\)), so its distribution piles up at zero, and in a
  coarse grid most tested brackets were inconclusive. Claimed deductions
  in full remain the target.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope and limits}\label{sec-cf-limits}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Partial equilibrium: \(K\), \(L\), \(\omega\), \(\varepsilon\) and
  \(M\) held fixed; only evasion responds. Large \(\Delta\) is outside
  what the exercise can speak to.
\item
  \(\theta\) fixed at one operating point that is not rejected
  (conservative test), with production-function instrument
  \(m^*_{it-1}\).
\item
  \(\hat\lambda\) is identified mostly from a thin tail of aggressive
  evaders; the estimate depends on the 0.5\% trim.
\end{itemize}

\section{Estimation mechanics}\label{estimation-mechanics}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  CUE quadratic objective with eigenvalue-truncated weighting matrix.
\item
  \textbf{Sales-tax revenue on sales is observed.} The data record the
  sales tax each firm pays on its sales in each period, which is the
  observed counterpart of \(\tau_{it}P_tY_{it}\) in
  Equation~\ref{eq-govt-rev}; total revenue in the counterfactual is
  computed from it directly, held at its baseline value.
\item
  \textbf{The \(\lambda\)-score moment enters in bounded form.} The
  score
  \(h_\lambda(e)\equiv\partial h(e;\lambda)/\partial\lambda=-2e/(1-2\lambda e)\)
  diverges near the FOC ceiling \(e\to1/(2\lambda)\), where a single
  extreme draw can dominate the weighting matrix's eigenvalues and
  silently collapse the effective moment count. The implementation
  therefore replaces \(h_\lambda\) with
  \(h_\lambda/(1-h_\lambda)\in(-1,0]\) in the ninth moment
  (\(1-2\lambda e\) floored at \(10^{-6}\)). Validity is unaffected: any
  function of \(e\) alone is independent of \(\varepsilon\) under the
  same argument that licenses the raw score. The transform decays
  polynomially, not exponentially, so firms stay numerically
  distinguishable across the whole range of \(e\); \(\exp(h_\lambda)\)
  and \(\tanh(h_\lambda)\) were rejected because both saturate to a
  constant for most of the sample.
\item
  Per-firm Metropolis chain: \(n_{burn}=1000\), \(n_{keep}=3000\) .
\item
  Nelder-Mead optimizer .
\item
  \(\gamma\) is unbounded in the optimizer (Schennach and AK2020 both
  treat \(\gamma\to\pm\infty\) as an expected solution, not a numerical
  failure).
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item

  fitting.

  \(\hat\lambda\) toward zero without it; 0.5\% is the first cutoff
  where the 95\% CI's upper bound stabilizes.
\end{itemize}

\section{Test inversion: the conservative test, and
why}\label{test-inversion-the-conservative-test-and-why}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Source definition (Schennach 2014, Definition F.1 and Theorem
  F.1):} with
  \(\hat g(\theta,\gamma)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\tilde g(Z_i,\theta,\gamma)\)
  (the sample average of the tilted moment from
  Equation~\ref{eq-elvis-tilt}) and
  \(\hat V(\theta,\gamma)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\tilde g(Z_i,\theta,\gamma)\tilde g(Z_i,\theta,\gamma)'\),
  her continuous updating objective is
  \(\hat L_n^S(\theta)=\sup_{\gamma\in\mathbb R^{d_g}}-\tfrac12\,\hat g(\theta,\gamma)'\hat V(\theta,\gamma)^{-}\hat g(\theta,\gamma)\),
  with \(\hat V^-\) a generalized inverse. Theorem F.1: at any
  \(\theta\) satisfying the moment condition, \(-2n\hat L_n^S(\theta)\)
  is asymptotically bounded above by \(\chi^2_{d_g}\). Maximizing
  \(\hat L_n^S\) over \(\gamma\) is the same as minimizing the
  nonnegative quadratic form \(\tfrac12\hat g'\hat V^-\hat g\); we
  report this positive quantity as \(\hat L_n(\theta)\), so
  \(TS(\theta)=2n\hat L_n(\theta)=-2n\hat L_n^S(\theta)\) --- same
  statistic, sign flipped purely so the optimizer minimizes a
  nonnegative objective instead of maximizing a nonpositive one.
  \(\hat V^-\) in our implementation discards eigen-directions with
  eigenvalues below \(10^{-8}\) times the largest, for numerical
  stability.
\item
  \textbf{Conservative test} (Theorem F.1, absolute):
  \(TS_{\text{cons}}=2n\hat L_n\) against \(\chi^2_{d_g,.95}\) directly
  -- no credit for profiling out nuisance parameters or \(\gamma\).
\item
  \textbf{Why the conservative test:} the counterfactual's purpose is to
  support a policy claim, so the confidence sets use the test whose size
  is guaranteed not to exceed its nominal level.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Second-Order Condition: Single-Tax
Model}\label{sec-app-soc-model}

\addcontentsline{loa}{appendixlist}{\appendixname~\thechapter: Second-Order Condition: Single-Tax Model}

This appendix derives the second-order condition (SOC) for the firm's
problem in Chapter~\ref{sec-model}, and shows how it translates into the
curvature restriction on the detection function \(q(e)\) used throughout
the paper.

\section{Setup}\label{setup-2}

Recall the firm's problem, Equation~\ref{eq-profit}, and its two
first-order conditions, Equation~\ref{eq-foc-m} and
Equation~\ref{eq-foc-e}. For a candidate solution
\((K_{it}^*,L_{it}^*,M_{it}^*,e_{it}^*)\) to be a maximum, the Hessian
of expected profit must be negative semi-definite at that point.

\section{The Hessian is
block-diagonal}\label{the-hessian-is-block-diagonal}

Two features of the model make the Hessian easy to sign.

First, \(M_{it}\perp e_{it}\): neither the materials FOC
(Equation~\ref{eq-foc-m}) nor the evasion FOC (Equation~\ref{eq-foc-e})
contains the other's choice variable, so every cross-partial between
\(e_{it}\) and \((K_{it},L_{it},M_{it})\) is zero. Second, the
production function
\(Y_{it}=K_{it}^{\alpha_K}L_{it}^{\alpha_L}M_{it}^\beta\exp(\omega_{it}+\varepsilon_{it})\)
is Cobb-Douglas, hence strictly concave in \((K_{it},L_{it},M_{it})\)
jointly. The Hessian is therefore block-diagonal,
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-app-soc-hessian}{
\nabla^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]=
\begin{bmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial e_{it}^2} & 0 & 0 & 0\\[4pt]
0 & \dfrac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial M_{it}^2} & \dfrac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial M_{it}\partial L_{it}} & \dfrac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial M_{it}\partial K_{it}}\\[4pt]
0 & \dfrac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial L_{it}\partial M_{it}} & \dfrac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial L_{it}^2} & \dfrac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial L_{it}\partial K_{it}}\\[4pt]
0 & \dfrac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial K_{it}\partial M_{it}} & \dfrac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial K_{it}\partial L_{it}} & \dfrac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial K_{it}^2}
\end{bmatrix}
}\end{equation}

with a single nonzero entry in the first row and column. Negative
definiteness requires the leading principal minors to alternate in sign
(odd-order minors negative, even-order positive). Since the lower
\(3\times3\) block is already negative definite (strict concavity of the
Cobb-Douglas production function in \(K_{it},L_{it},M_{it}\)), the
\emph{only} condition the evasion margin adds to the firm's problem is
that the first leading principal minor --- the top-left scalar entry ---
is itself negative: \[
\frac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial e_{it}^2}<0.
\]

The capital, labour, and materials choices place no restriction on the
evasion technology, and vice versa; the SOC for \(e_{it}\) can be
analyzed entirely on its own.

\section{The scalar condition}\label{the-scalar-condition}

Differentiating the evasion FOC (Equation~\ref{eq-foc-e}) once more with
respect to \(e_{it}\),
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-app-soc-full}{
\frac{\partial^2\mathbb{E}[\pi_{it}]}{\partial e_{it}^2}=-\tau\rho_t(1+\phi)\Big(2q'(e_{it})+e_{it}q''(e_{it})\Big)-\frac{\partial^2\kappa_{it}(e_{it},\omega_{it})}{\partial e_{it}^2}<0.
}\end{equation}

Rearranging, the SOC holds whenever \[
\tau\rho_t(1+\phi)\Big(2q'(e_{it})+e_{it}q''(e_{it})\Big)+\frac{\partial^2\kappa_{it}(e_{it},\omega_{it})}{\partial e_{it}^2}>0,
\] a sum of two terms. It is therefore \emph{sufficient} --- not
necessary --- that either term alone be strictly positive:
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-app-soc-split}{
\frac{\partial^2\kappa_{it}(e_{it},\omega_{it})}{\partial e_{it}^2}\ge0
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
\tau\rho_t(1+\phi)\Big(2q'(e_{it})+e_{it}q''(e_{it})\Big)\ge0.
}\end{equation}

\textbf{Under this paper's headline cost function},
\(\kappa_{it}(e_{it},\omega_{it})=e_{it}\exp\{\delta_0-\delta_1\omega_{it}+\delta_2\omega_{it}^2+\psi_{it}\}\)
--- linear in \(e_{it}\) --- the first term is identically zero,
\(\partial^2\kappa_{it}/\partial e_{it}^2=0\). The cost function
therefore contributes nothing toward satisfying the SOC on its own; the
entire burden falls on the second condition, the curvature of the
detection function.

\section{The curvature restriction}\label{the-curvature-restriction}

Since \(\tau\rho_t(1+\phi)>0\), the second condition in
Equation~\ref{eq-app-soc-split} reduces to
\(2q'(e_{it})+e_{it}q''(e_{it})\ge0\). Economic theory imposes
\(q'(e_{it})>0\) (detection is increasing in overreporting) and
\(e_{it}>0\) for an interior evader, so dividing through by
\(q'(e_{it})e_{it}>0\) gives
\begin{equation}\protect\phantomsection\label{eq-app-soc-final}{
-\frac{q''(e_{it})}{q'(e_{it})}\,e_{it}<2.
}\end{equation}

This is a restriction on the \textbf{curvature of \(q\) alone} --- a
firm's evasion cost never enters, given the linear-in-\(e\)
specification above. It says \(q'\) may decline as \(e_{it}\) rises (a
concave detection function), but not too fast relative to its own level:
the semi-elasticity of the marginal detection probability with respect
to \(e_{it}\) must exceed \(-2\).

\textbf{Linear detection, \(q(e)=\min\{\lambda e,1\}\)} (headline
specification): \(q''(e)=0\) on the interior, so \(-q''e/q'=0<2\)

restriction on \(\lambda e\) beyond the FOC's own ceiling
(\(\lambda e<1/2\), from Chapter~\ref{sec-model}).

\textbf{Concave detection, \(q(e)=1-e^{-\lambda e}\)}:
\(q'(e)=\lambda e^{-\lambda e}\) and
\(q''(e)=-\lambda^2e^{-\lambda e}\), so \(-q''e/q'=\lambda e\). The SOC

for this functional form, looser than) the FOC's own ceiling
\(\lambda e<1\) derived in Chapter~\ref{sec-model}. Since
\(\lambda e<1\implies\lambda e<2\), the FOC ceiling is the binding
constraint in practice; the SOC is automatically satisfied wherever the
FOC has an interior solution at all.

\textbf{Convex detection, \(q(e)=\min\{(\lambda e)^k,1\}\), \(k>1\)}:
\(q'(e)=k\lambda(\lambda e)^{k-1}\) and
\(q''(e)=k(k-1)\lambda^2(\lambda e)^{k-2}\), so \(-q''e/q'=1-k\). Since

convex detection satisfies the SOC unconditionally, for any \(k>1\).

second-order condition on the relevant part of their domain --- linear
and convex unconditionally, concave provided \(\lambda e<2\), which is
already implied by that function's own FOC-ceiling. The choice among
them is therefore not disciplined by the SOC; it is a modelling choice
about the detection technology, addressed on economic grounds in
Chapter~\ref{sec-model}.

\chapter{The Tax Wedge in the Reform
Estimates}\label{sec-app-fiscal-wedge}

\addcontentsline{loa}{appendixlist}{\appendixname~\thechapter: The Tax Wedge in the Reform Estimates}

A tax change can move reported materials shares mechanically, without
any change in overreporting: prices and the tax paid on sales and
purchases adjust to the new rates. The two-tax model isolates the part
of this adjustment that runs through the sales-tax rates. With different
rates on sales, \(\tau_S\), and on purchases, \(\tau_P\), the gross
materials share carries the wedge
\(\ln\big((1-\tau_S)/(1-\tau_P)\big)\), which depends on the rates
themselves. The 1983 reform changed those rates in the liable
industries, so a rise in the gross share after the reform could reflect
either more overreporting or this wedge. The event study in
Chapter~\ref{sec-fiscal} uses the share net of sales taxes, which
removes the wedge.

the gross share and on the net share. Before the reform the wedge does
not move. After it, the wedge rises by about 0.006 to 0.008 for
unincorporated firms in liable industries, roughly a tenth of their
measured response, and by 0.011 to 0.013 for proprietorships in
1984--1986, most of their response in those years. An event study on the
