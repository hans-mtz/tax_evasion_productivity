# RAP drafts for the conclusion (ch. 10), 24 Sept 2026

Purpose: three candidate arguments for the conclusion, stressing what the method delivers that existing approaches cannot, without overselling. Claims about other methods are limited to what Slemrod (2019, JEL) supports; page numbers given.

## What the literature supports about other methods

| Claim | Support | Use it? |
|---|---|---|
| Governments randomize letters, audits, information reporting, but not tax rates | Slemrod 2019, pp. 913, 942 | Yes |
| Field experiments need a partner government; results are hard to scale up and to generalize | pp. 913, 942 ("randomization bias", scaling from pilot to population) | Yes |
| Recent work relies on administrative tax data and third-party reports, available under access protocols in a limited set of countries | pp. 913 (§3.3), 926–927 | Yes |
| Random audits measure detected, not true, evasion; programs are rare | §3.1, §4.1 | Yes, carefully |
| Letter experiments are expensive | Relative to *what*: Slemrod calls letters inexpensive for a tax authority (p. 938), but against working from existing production data they are a large enterprise (partner authority, design, sending, collecting responses, before/after comparison), costly above all in time | **Yes, as a comparison** with downloading survey data, not as a general claim (Hans, 2026-09-24) |
| Firms adjust reported costs when enforcement tightens | p. 927 (96 cents; 70–80 cents) | Yes |

## Advantages of the method (the material for P and A)

1. **Data:** needs only firm-level production data (survey or administrative) plus a group of firms that credibly do not evade. Production surveys are the standard input of the productivity literature (e.g. the Colombian EAM used by GNR 2020); administrative data work too if available.
2. **No government partnership or experiment:** can be applied to past periods (here 1981–1991, before administrative tax data were accessible) and to settings where authorities do not cooperate.
3. **Level and distribution, not only a response:** recovers how much firms overreport relative to their true inputs, firm heterogeneity included, not only the change induced by an intervention.
4. **Tax rates, not only enforcement:** combined with a statutory reform and a structural model, it speaks to how evasion responds to rates, the margin experiments cannot vary.
5. **Corrects production and productivity estimates** as a by-product.

Honest limits to keep next to these: the non-evader group is an assumption; failures to reject are inconclusive; results cover selected industries.

## RAP 1 (lead with measurement)

| Element | Draft |
|---|---|
| **P** | Most of what we know about tax evasion comes from audits, administrative records and field experiments that depend on a government's cooperation and vary enforcement rather than tax rates [@Slemrod2019], so evasion remains hard to measure wherever such access is missing. |
| **R** | Can input-cost overreporting be measured from firms' production data alone? |
| **A** | Yes. When some firms credibly do not evade, their input shares identify the technology, and the excess reported by other firms identifies overreporting and its distribution. In Colombia, overreporting is detected in 8 of 20 industries, and in five industries firms overreport 11–25% of their true materials. |

| Check | Verdict |
|---|---|
| P makes space for R | Yes: P says measurement depends on access and cooperation; R asks whether it can be done without them. |
| P leads idly to A | Yes: "hard to measure without access" → "how, then?" → A gives the mechanism and the numbers. |
| A answers R | Yes: yes/no question, answered "yes", then how and how much. |

Risk: sells the method, undersells the policy result; reform and counterfactual come after as supporting findings.

## RAP 2 (lead with tax rates and policy)

| Element | Draft |
|---|---|
| **P** | Governments are willing to randomize letters and audits but not tax rates [@Slemrod2019, pp. 913, 942], so we know how firms' reported costs respond to enforcement [@Carrillo2017] but little about how they respond to the tax rate itself. |
| **R** | How does cost overreporting respond to tax rates, and what does that imply for the revenue effect of a rate change? |
| **A** | Overreporting responds to incentives: after the 1983 reform it rose where the net incentive rose most. The estimated model implies an asymmetric response: a 0.5% rate increase already raises claimed deductions by 16–28%, while a cut must reach 8% before claims fall detectably (by 6–16%). |

| Check | Verdict |
|---|---|
| P makes space for R | Yes: enforcement is studied, rates are not; R asks about rates. |
| P leads idly to A | Yes: "rates are never randomized" → "so how did you get at it?" → reform plus structural model. |
| A answers R | Yes: "how" answered by direction (responds) and shape (asymmetric), then the implication. |

Risk: the method becomes a means, and its advantages appear only implicitly. Matches the chosen headline (evasion and tax policy first).

## RAP 3 (lead with the method as a tool, both measurement and policy)

| Element | Draft |
|---|---|
| **P** | Evidence on evasion comes mostly from settings where governments open their records or run experiments, which are costly to arrange, rarely available for the past, and silent on tax rates [@Slemrod2019]. |
| **R** | Can production data, which researchers already routinely use, measure input overreporting and its response to tax rates? |
| **A** | Yes. With a group of non-evading firms, production data reveal how much firms overreport (11–25% of true materials in five Colombian industries), show that overreporting rose after the 1983 rate increase where incentives rose most, and support a model in which small rate increases raise claimed deductions sharply while cuts recover little. |

| Check | Verdict |
|---|---|
| P makes space for R | Yes: P lists what current approaches need and cannot do; R asks whether commonly used data can do it. |
| P leads idly to A | Yes: "most evidence needs government access" → "is there another way?" → A. |
| A answers R | Yes, "yes", followed by what the data deliver on both counts. |

Risk: "costly to arrange" must be framed as cooperation and access, not money (see table above). Longest A; needs discipline in the paragraph.

## Recommendation

RAP 3 fits the conclusion best: it states the advantages you want (data already in use, no partnership needed, works for the past, speaks to rates) and carries both the measurement and the policy findings. RAP 2 fits the intro's opening better, given the evasion-and-policy headline; the conclusion can then close the loop with RAP 3. RAP 1 is the fallback if the policy results are the ones a referee is likely to question.

Outline changes if RAP 3 is chosen: P1 becomes P + R; P2 is A in three parts (measurement, response to the 1983 reform, counterfactual) plus productivity as a by-product; P3 lists the advantages (points 1–4 above); P4 limits; P5 next steps.


**Decision (Hans, 2026-09-24):** RAP 3.
