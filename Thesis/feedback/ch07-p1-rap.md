# RAP review — Chapter 7, first paragraph

Source: `Thesis/chapters/07-fiscal-policy.qmd`, paragraph under `<!-- P1: what is the question of this section -->`.

> In 1983, Colombia increased the sales tax (ST) rate on most manufacturing industries, while reducing income-tax (IT) rates differently for proprietorships and LLCs. I investigate whether firms responded by overreporting more inputs. Evidence suggest overreporting by unincorporated firms escalated after the reform reaching about 8% of true materials by 1987. Overreporting expanded the most among firms whose net incentives expanded the most.

## Implied RAP

| Element | Extracted text | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| **P** | "In 1983, Colombia increased the sales tax (ST) rate on most manufacturing industries, while reducing income-tax (IT) rates differently for proprietorships and LLCs." | This describes an event, not a gap in knowledge: it says nothing about what is already known or what would be useful to know. The chapter's actual positioning statement is in paragraph 2: "Because these studies vary enforcement, less is known about whether input overreporting also responds to tax rates." |
| **R** | "I investigate whether firms responded by overreporting more inputs." | A *Does…?* question, but tied to one reform, and "firms" is broader than the group the answer covers. The general version readers would ask is "Does input overreporting respond to tax rates?" Possible audiences: (i) public-finance and tax-compliance empiricists (Slemrod-style literature on rates vs. enforcement); (ii) VAT and sales-tax design and policy readers, who care about fictitious purchase credits; (iii) your committee, reading this chapter as motivating evidence for the structural model's identification of $\lambda$ from $\tau\rho_t$. The current wording speaks most to (i) and to Colombia specialists, and least to (iii). |
| **A** | "Evidence suggest overreporting by unincorporated firms escalated after the reform reaching about 8% of true materials by 1987. Overreporting expanded the most among firms whose net incentives expanded the most." | Two findings, a level and a gradient. Neither is stated as a higher-level idea. The higher-level message is implicit: *overreporting follows the net tax incentive*. The "8% by 1987" figure is a detail that could support A rather than be A. Intended readers are likely to accept "evasion rises with the rate" and "evasion tracks net incentives" as the abstractions. Watch "escalated": it implies acceleration, not just an increase. |

## Coherence checks

| Check | Verdict | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Does P make space for R? | **Partially** | From sentence 1, readers can infer *that* R is askable (a reform happened, so did firms respond?), but not *why* it is worth asking given the literature. The gap (enforcement is well studied, rates are not) arrives only in paragraph 2, so the first paragraph's P doesn't do its job alone. |
| Does P idly lead readers to A? | **Partially** | The juxtaposition is good material for idle curiosity: the sales-tax rate went *up* while the income-tax rate went *down*, by different amounts for different firms, so readers wonder which effect wins. A's second sentence ("net incentives") satisfies exactly that curiosity. But the link that makes the income-tax cut relevant is missing: overreporting inputs lowers *both* taxes (a larger purchase credit and larger deductible costs). Without that link, the income-tax clause reads as a side detail, and "net incentives" appears in A without having been set up in P. |
| Does A answer R? | **Partially** | R is a *Does…?* question, so readers expect "yes" or "no", but A never commits to one; "evidence suggests… escalated" is only an implied yes. There's also a scope mismatch: R asks about "firms", while A answers for "unincorporated firms", and the corporate benchmark that explains this restriction is only introduced in paragraph 3. Finally, A's second sentence answers a different R (the gradient across firms), not the one asked. |

## Alternative RAPs

These are argument sketches to test the structure, not replacement prose.

| Alternative | Does P make space for R? | Does P idly lead readers to A? | Does A answer R? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 — Literature-gap framing.** *P:* Firms are known to overreport costs when enforcement tightens, but whether input overreporting also responds to tax rates is largely unknown, because governments rarely change rates in ways researchers can exploit. *R:* Does input overreporting rise when tax rates rise? *A:* Yes. After Colombia's 1983 sales-tax increase, overreporting by unincorporated firms rose, reaching about 8% of true materials by 1987. This moves paragraph 2's gap sentence up front and keeps the reform as the *setting* for A rather than as P. | **Yes.** P states what is known (enforcement responses) and what is not (rate responses), so R follows directly as the missing piece. | **Yes.** "Largely unknown… rarely change rates" makes readers wonder whether it does, and A opens by answering that. | **Yes.** A *Does* question is answered with "Yes". R and A share the same scope once "unincorporated firms" is named in A, or R says "firms that can evade". |
| **2 — Net-incentive framing.** *P:* Overreporting inputs lowers both sales and income taxes, so when Colombia's 1983 reform raised the sales-tax rate but cut income-tax rates by different amounts for proprietorships and LLCs, it changed the net gain from overreporting by different amounts across firms, and existing evidence, which varies enforcement rather than rates, cannot say whether evasion follows such changes. *R:* Does input overreporting follow the net tax incentive to overreport? *A:* Yes. Overreporting by unincorporated firms rose after the reform, to about 8% of true materials by 1987, and rose most where the net incentive rose most. | **Yes**, though P is long. The mechanism clause sets up "net incentive", and the closing clause supplies the literature gap. P could be split into two sentences if a single sentence is too heavy. | **Yes, strongly.** Opposing rate changes of different sizes create exactly the "which effect wins, and for whom?" curiosity, and A's gradient answers it. | **Yes.** "Yes" answers the *Does* question, and both of A's findings (the level rise, and the larger rise where incentives rose more) are evidence that overreporting follows the net incentive. This alternative uses your current four sentences' content most fully, including the heterogeneity result. |

Alternative 2 fits the chapter's later paragraphs best, since paragraph 3 already builds the proprietorship/LLC/corporation contrast around net incentives. Alternative 1 is the simpler, more general hook for a first-time reader.
