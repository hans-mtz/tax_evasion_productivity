## Two alternative RAPs for the new focus

**Alternative 1 (measurement and fiscal cost; public finance).** P: firms' cost overreporting has been measured only with confidential tax records in a few countries [@Carrillo2022; @Zumaya2021], and validation-sample methods that use survey data were built for individuals, whose indirect measure of true income does not depend on productivity [@Pissarides1989; @Gorodnichenko2009]. R: can firms' input overreporting be detected and measured from standard production data, and how much does it cost the government? A: yes; with corporations as a truthful benchmark for the common technology, a test valid for any detection and cost function detects overreporting in about half of the liable industries and in neither exempt one, deconvolution puts it at 4.5 to 23.1 percent of true materials, and a structural model of the evasion decision puts its cost at 4.7 percent of gross sales-tax revenue from these firms.

**Alternative 2 (the confound; public finance and productivity).** P: methods that infer productivity from reported inputs treat inputs as true [@Gandhi2020; @Ackerberg2015], while evidence on firm evasion comes from tax records; for firms, overreported inputs and low productivity look the same in the data. R: how can input overreporting be separated from productivity, and what does separating them reveal? A: by benchmarking the technology on firms that report truthfully; doing so shows that overreporting is common, large and costly to the government, and that ignoring it makes materials look more productive and productivity more dispersed and less persistent.
